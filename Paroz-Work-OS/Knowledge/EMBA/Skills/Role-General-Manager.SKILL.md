## Core Frameworks

### Framework 1: Strategy Definition — The Integrated Set of Choices

Strategy is not a single decision but an **integrated set of choices** that answer six fundamental questions:

| Question | What It Addresses |
|----------|------------------|
| **Why we exist** | Purpose and mission |
| **What we do** | Products, services, and activities |
| **For whom** | Target customers and markets |
| **How we make money** | Revenue model and value capture |
| **How we deliver** | Operating model, capabilities, and processes |
| **When / Sequence** | Timing and sequencing of strategic moves |

**What strategy provides:**
- **Continuity** — stable direction amidst operational noise
- **Focus** — clarity on what to pursue and what to decline
- **Enables tough choices** — framework for saying no
- **Integrates functions** — aligns marketing, operations, finance, HR
- **Translates ideas to action** — bridges vision and execution

---

### Framework 2: Environmental Assessment

The external half of the Strategic Assessment framework. Three concentric levels of analysis:

#### Level 1: Macro Environment (PEST)

| Factor | What to Scan |
|--------|-------------|
| **Political** | Government policy, stability, trade regulations, taxation, political risk |
| **Economic** | Growth rates, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, business cycle position |
| **Social** | Demographics, lifestyle trends, education, cultural attitudes, labor mobility |
| **Technological** | Innovation pace, R&D activity, automation, digital transformation, disruptive technologies |

#### Level 2: Industry Analysis

Assess the structural attractiveness of the industry — competitive intensity, barriers to entry, supplier and buyer dynamics, substitute threats, and the overall profit potential of the sector.

#### Level 3: Competitive Analysis

Direct assessment of key competitors — their strategies, resources, capabilities, and likely moves.

#### Four Levels of Uncertainty

| Level | Description | Strategic Approach |
|-------|------------|-------------------|
| **1: Clear Future** | Single reliable forecast of the future | Standard strategic planning |
| **2: Alternate Futures** | Small number of discrete outcomes | Scenario planning; contingent strategies |
| **3: Range of Futures** | Continuous range of possible outcomes | Robust strategies that perform across the range |
| **4: Unpredictable** | No basis for forecasting | Adaptive strategies; strategic options; flexibility |

#### Stakeholder Analysis

Identify all parties with a stake in the organization's outcomes — shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, regulators, community — and assess their interests, power, and potential impact on strategy.

---

### Framework 3: Organizational Assessment

The internal half of the Strategic Assessment framework. Four dimensions:

| Dimension | What to Assess |
|-----------|---------------|
| **People** | Leadership capability, talent depth, culture, organizational structure, skills gaps |
| **Money** | Financial health, capital availability, cost structure, investment capacity |
| **Technology** | Systems, infrastructure, digital capabilities, proprietary technology, IT maturity |
| **Information** | Data assets, knowledge management, decision-support systems, competitive intelligence |

**Combined Strategic Assessment Flow:**

```
Environmental Assessment          Organizational Assessment
(Macro + Industry + Competitive)  (People + Money + Technology + Information)
              \                              /
               \                            /
                → Strategic Issues ←
                        |
                    Direction
        (Vision, Mission, Objectives,
         Strategies, Scorecard)
```

### Framework 4: Strategic Planning Presentation Template

The comprehensive template for creating an actionable strategic plan:

1. **Executive Summary** — crisp overview of the strategic direction
2. **Environmental Factors** — macro PEST trends shaping the landscape
3. **Industry Factors** — structural forces and industry dynamics
4. **Competitive Factors** — competitor positioning and moves
5. **Strategic Issues (SWOT)** — synthesis of external and internal assessment
6. **Strategic/Operational Health Matrix** — current state diagnosis
7. **Core Competences** — distinctive capabilities the organization can leverage
8. **Culture Assessment** — organizational values, norms, and alignment
9. **Vision, Mission, and Objectives** — aspirational direction and measurable goals
10. **Positioning** — where the organization plays and how it wins
11. **KPI Scorecard** — metrics to track strategic progress
12. **Strategies and Action Plans** — specific initiatives to execute the strategy
13. **Resource Requirements** — capital, people, and technology needed
14. **GANTT Chart** — timeline and sequencing of strategic initiatives
15. **HR Requirements** — talent acquisition, development, and organizational changes
16. **Financial Impact** — projected P&L, ROI, and financial outcomes
17. **Major Risks** — identification and mitigation of strategic risks

### Framework 5: The GM's Day Job — Running the Business

**The 80-Hour Week:**

| Time Allocation | Hours | Activities |
|-----------------|-------|-----------|
| **Operational** | ~60 hours | Budget management, HR management, meetings, fire-fighting, operations oversight, communications, sales support, board management, stakeholder relations, community engagement |
| **Strategic** | ~20 hours | Building for the future — strategic planning, change initiatives, long-term positioning |

**Mintzberg's Findings on What Managers Really Do:**
- Work is characterized by brevity, variety, and fragmentation
- Managers prefer live action and verbal communication
- Much of the work is reactive (fire-fighting) rather than proactive
- Strategic reflection competes with operational demands for attention

**The Core Tension:** Build for the future while managing today. The GM must protect strategic time against the relentless pull of operational urgency.

### Framework 6: Strategic vs Operational Planning

| Dimension | Strategic Planning | Operational Planning |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Time Horizon** | 3-5-10 years | 12 months |
| **Budget Type** | Investment budgets | Operating budgets |
| **Focus** | Projects for change | Routine procedures |
| **Nature** | Transformational | Incremental |
| **Output** | New capabilities, market positions, competitive advantages | Efficient execution of current business model |

**Key Principle:** These are two differentiated yet linked processes. Strategic plans drive operational plans, but operational realities inform and constrain strategic ambitions. The GM must manage both simultaneously without conflating them.

---

### Framework 7: Change and Execution — Six Takeaways

1. **Strategic planning is the game plan for change** — it defines what must be different
2. **Projects are the vehicle for change** — strategy is executed through discrete, resourced projects
3. **Timing and sequencing are critical** — the order in which initiatives are launched determines success
4. **Establish winning conditions** — set up for success before launching change
5. **Execution is key** — strategy without execution is aspiration; execution determines outcomes
6. **Disciplined leadership is required** — sustained commitment, accountability, and follow-through

#### Why Change Fails

| Failure Mode | Description |
|-------------|-------------|
| **Too many initiatives** | Spreading resources and attention too thin |
| **Wrong issues** | Addressing symptoms rather than root causes |
| **Silos** | Functional boundaries block cross-organizational collaboration |
| **Fear** | Resistance born from anxiety about the unknown |
| **Complacency** | Satisfaction with the status quo despite external threats |
| **Under-resourcing** | Committing to change without adequate investment |
| **Cynicism** | Past failed initiatives breed skepticism about new ones |

### Framework 8: Discovery-Driven Planning (DDP)

Discovery-Driven Planning is a disciplined approach to planning under uncertainty, developed by McGrath and MacMillan (HBR). Unlike conventional planning — which starts with confident forecasts and builds execution plans around them — DDP acknowledges that key assumptions are unknown and designs a process to systematically test them before committing major resources.

**Core Principle:** In high-uncertainty initiatives, the goal of planning is not to prove a predetermined plan will work, but to identify and test the assumptions that must hold true for it to succeed.

#### Reverse Income Statement

Start from the required financial outcome and work backward to the operational metrics that must be achieved:

| Step | Question | Output |
|------|----------|--------|
| 1. Required Profits | What profit level justifies the investment? | Target net income |
| 2. Required Revenue | What revenue is needed to deliver that profit (given estimated cost structure)? | Revenue target |
| 3. Required Market Share | What share of the addressable market does that revenue represent? | Market share % |
| 4. Required Unit Sales | How many units/customers must be acquired to reach that share? | Unit volume target |
| 5. Required Pricing | What price point supports the revenue target at that volume? | Price per unit |

The reverse income statement forces planners to confront whether the required numbers are plausible before investing.

#### Assumption Checklist

Every plan rests on assumptions. DDP requires making them explicit, then prioritizing which to test first:

1. **List all assumptions** — enumerate every assumption embedded in the plan (market size, customer willingness to pay, cost to acquire, development timeline, regulatory approval, etc.)
2. **Rank by importance** — which assumptions, if wrong, would destroy the business case?
3. **Rank by uncertainty** — which assumptions do we have the least evidence for?
4. **Test most critical first** — focus resources on validating assumptions that are both highly important AND highly uncertain (the "assumption danger zone")

```
                    HIGH IMPORTANCE
                         │
        ─────────────────┼─────────────────
        Safe Assumptions │ ASSUMPTION
        (important but   │ DANGER ZONE
         well-evidenced) │ (test these first)
                         │
  LOW UNCERTAINTY ───────┼──────── HIGH UNCERTAINTY
                         │
        Low Priority     │ Worth Monitoring
        (unimportant,    │ (uncertain but low
         well-evidenced) │  impact if wrong)
                         │
                    LOW IMPORTANCE
```

#### Milestone Planning

Define checkpoints where key assumptions are validated before committing additional resources:

| Milestone | Assumption Being Tested | Evidence Required | Decision |
|-----------|------------------------|-------------------|----------|
| Milestone 1 | Customer problem exists and is worth solving | Customer interviews, willingness-to-pay data | Continue / Pivot / Kill |
| Milestone 2 | Solution can be built within cost envelope | Prototype cost and timeline data | Continue / Pivot / Kill |
| Milestone 3 | Customers will pay at the required price point | Pilot revenue, conversion rates | Continue / Pivot / Kill |
| Milestone 4 | Unit economics support scaling | Contribution margin, CAC, LTV data | Scale / Pivot / Kill |

At each milestone, the team explicitly decides: **Continue** (assumptions validated, commit more resources), **Pivot** (assumptions partially validated, redirect approach), or **Kill** (assumptions invalidated, stop investment).

#### When to Use DDP

| Situation | Why DDP Applies |
|-----------|----------------|
| **High-uncertainty ventures** | Key assumptions about market, technology, or economics are unproven |
| **New market entry** | Limited data on customer behavior, competitive dynamics, or regulatory landscape |
| **Innovation projects** | Novel products or services where demand and feasibility are uncertain |
| **Digital transformation** | Technology adoption, organizational change, and ROI assumptions are speculative |
| **Strategic investments with irreversible commitments** | Large capital outlays that are difficult to reverse if assumptions fail |

#### DDP vs. Conventional Planning

| Dimension | Conventional Planning | Discovery-Driven Planning |
|-----------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Starting point** | Revenue forecast based on market projections | Required profits, working backward to required assumptions |
| **Treatment of assumptions** | Implicit, often unexamined | Explicit, ranked, and systematically tested |
| **Resource commitment** | Upfront, based on the plan | Incremental, tied to milestone validation |
| **Response to new information** | Plan is defended; deviations are "problems" | Plan is updated; deviations are "learning" |
| **Decision gates** | Annual review or budget cycle | Milestone-based checkpoints at each assumption test |
| **Success metric** | Plan accuracy (did we hit the forecast?) | Learning velocity (how fast did we validate/invalidate assumptions?) |
| **Risk profile** | High — full commitment before uncertainty is resolved | Low — investment scales with evidence |
