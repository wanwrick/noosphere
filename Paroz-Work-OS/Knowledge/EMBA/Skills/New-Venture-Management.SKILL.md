## Module 1: Being Entrepreneurial

### Entrepreneurs vs. Intrapreneurs

| Dimension | Entrepreneur | Intrapreneur |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| **Who** | Sets up own enterprise | Employed, works on something new within organization |
| **Resources** | Finds own resources | Uses company resources |
| **Capital** | Own capital or funded | Financed by company |
| **Org Form** | New business | Existing/new enterprise within existing company |
| **Risk** | Borne by entrepreneur | Borne by company |
| **Permission** | From yourself | Seek permission from company |

### Startup Ecosystem Success Factors

What makes a great startup ecosystem? Key scoring dimensions include talent, funding access, regulatory environment, market access, infrastructure, culture, and support networks.

**Rankings:** US #1, Canada #4, Mexico #41 (Global Entrepreneurship Index)

### Tales of Three Startups

- **Shopify:** Frustration-driven (bad e-commerce platforms), organic growth + key acquisitions, Ottawa-based
- **Slack:** Pivot from failed game (Glitch) to internal comms tool, 8000 customers in <24 hours, acquired by Salesforce for $28B
- **Wealthsimple:** Spreadsheet tips for colleagues → $38.7B AUM fintech, merged with ShareOwner for instant broker-dealer access

### Side Hustles

Side hustles enrich full-time work performance. Employees with side hustles feel more empowered (self-determination, impact, competence, meaning) and have more energy.

## Module 2: Ideas & Opportunities

### The Entrepreneurial Process

```
Idea → Opportunity → Business "Plan" → Startup → Growth
(Smoke) (Potential)   (Virtual Reality) (Reality) (Hyper Reality)
```

### Finding Ideas — Your Radar Screen

Key sources: Podcasts, industry reports, "burning" problems, converging trends, creativity, existing businesses, franchises, patents, product licensing, corporations, universities, trade contacts, professional contacts, travel, GenAI tools.

**Critical distinction:** All opportunities start with an idea, but not all ideas are good business opportunities.

### First-Level Opportunity Screening Framework

| Dimension | What to Assess |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Problem/Customer** | Identified and understood? Worth solving? Large enough market? Growing market? |
| **Timing** | Macro factors favorable? Industry factors favorable? Competitive forces favorable? Limited complexity? |
| **Money Making** | More than 1 revenue stream? Big fat margins? Cash flows early? Market worthwhile? |
| **Team** | Know it all? Know everyone? Are known for everything? Appropriate for this opportunity? |

### Screening Out Losers

Potential and superior opportunities share:
- Create/add significant value — solve customer "pain"
- Timing is good
- Robust/forgiving market, margin, and moneymaking characteristics
- Good fit with founder(s) and key people

## Module 4: New Venture Business Planning

### Planning as Risk Reduction

Good new venture business planning is a **risk reduction exercise + game plan for launch**.

### Two Schools of Thought

| For Formal Planning | Against Formal Planning |
|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Forces thorough thinking | "Lean" startup — time better spent executing |
| Critical for raising money and convincing stakeholders | No strong link between plan and success |
| Forces management discipline early | Easy to over-plan to avoid taking risks |
| Ultimate de-risking and team-building | Confusion about what "business plan" means |

**Reality:** "No plan survives first contact with the customer." It's not about the document — it's about the planning process.

### From Opportunity to Business Plan

```
Opportunity Screen → Sit Down & Plan (a bit) → Test & Learn
```

### Business Planning Decisions

| Type | Nature |
|------|--------|
| **Strategic** | BIG decisions (market, positioning, business model) |
| **Tactical** | SMALLER decisions (operations, pricing, channels) |

Format: Rarely lengthy narrative; more commonly evolving slide decks.

## Module 5: Hypothesis-Driven Entrepreneurship (Lean Startup)

### Core Framework (Eisenmann, Ries, Dillard — HBS)

**Process Steps:**
1. **Develop a Vision** — Ideation for the problem and potential solution
2. **Translate Vision into Hypotheses** — Falsifiable business model hypotheses
3. **Test with MVPs** — Minimum Viable Product = smallest set of activities to disprove a hypothesis
4. **Act on Feedback** — Persevere, Pivot, or Perish
5. **Repeat** until product-market fit achieved

**"Lean" means:** Avoiding waste (like lean manufacturing), NOT bootstrapping on a shoestring. Lean startups may eventually invest enormous capital — but only after validation.

### Four Startup Approaches Compared

| Approach | Direction | Feedback |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Build-It-And-They-Will-Come** | Strong (founder vision) | None |
| **Waterfall Planning** | Strong (sequential phases) | Limited |
| **Just Do It!** | Weak (improvisational) | Strong (but undirected) |
| **Lean Startup** | Strong (hypothesis-driven) | Strong (structured MVP tests) |

### Business Model Hypothesis Framework

Four elements to translate into testable hypotheses:

| Element | Key Questions |
|---------|-------------|
| **Customer Value Proposition** | Unmet needs? Target segments? Differentiation or low cost? MVP at launch? Pricing? |
| **Go-To-Market Plan** | Direct vs. indirect channels? CAC targets? Free vs. paid demand generation? Viral coefficient? Chasm crossing? |
| **Technology & Operations** | Build vs. buy? Key technical risks? Operations scalability? |
| **Cash Flow Formula** | Revenue model? Unit economics? Breakeven timeline? Funding requirements? |

### SaaS Unit Economics

Unit economics are the foundation of any recurring-revenue business model. Investors and founders must understand these metrics to evaluate sustainability and growth potential.

**Key Formulas:**

| Metric | Formula | Interpretation |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| **LTV (Lifetime Value)** | ARPU × Gross Margin / Churn Rate | Total gross profit from an average customer over their lifetime |
| **CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)** | Total Sales & Marketing Spend / New Customers Acquired | Fully loaded cost to acquire one new customer |
| **LTV/CAC Ratio** | LTV / CAC | ≥ 3:1 is healthy; < 1.5 is a red flag |
| **CAC Payback Period** | CAC / (ARPU × Gross Margin) | Months to recover acquisition cost; < 18 months is healthy |
| **Monthly Churn Rate** | Lost Customers in Period / Customers at Start of Period | Lower is better; < 2% monthly for SMB, < 1% for enterprise |
| **Net Revenue Retention (NRR)** | (Starting MRR + Expansion − Contraction − Churn) / Starting MRR | > 100% means growth from existing customers alone; > 120% is elite |
| **Rule of 40** | Revenue Growth % + Profit Margin % ≥ 40 | Combined health metric; above 40 signals a strong SaaS business |
| **Burn Multiple** | Net Burn / Net New ARR | How much cash is burned per dollar of new ARR; < 1.5x is efficient |

**Benchmark Ranges by Stage:**

| Stage | ARR Range | LTV/CAC | CAC Payback | Monthly Churn | NRR | Rule of 40 |
|-------|-----------|---------|-------------|---------------|-----|------------|
| **Pre-Seed / Seed** | $0–$1M | ≥ 1.5:1 (acceptable) | < 24 months | < 5% | > 80% | Not yet applicable |
| **Series A** | $1M–$5M | ≥ 3:1 | < 18 months | < 3% | > 100% | Emerging |
| **Series B** | $5M–$20M | ≥ 3:1 | < 12 months | < 2% | > 110% | ≥ 30 |
| **Growth / Series C+** | $20M+ | ≥ 4:1 | < 12 months | < 1.5% | > 120% | ≥ 40 |

**Quick Worked Example:**
- ARPU = $500/month, Gross Margin = 80%, Monthly Churn = 2%
- LTV = $500 × 0.80 / 0.02 = **$20,000**
- If CAC = $5,000 → LTV/CAC = 4:1 (healthy)
- CAC Payback = $5,000 / ($500 × 0.80) = **12.5 months** (healthy)

> **Cross-reference:** For SaaS valuation multiples (EV/Revenue, EV/ARR) and comparable company analysis, see **Valuation.SKILL.md** — particularly the market-based valuation approaches and industry-specific multiples for technology companies.

## Angel/Seed Investor Evaluation Framework

This section provides the investor perspective on new venture evaluation — complementing the entrepreneur-centric frameworks above. Whether evaluating a startup for investment, advising founders, or preparing for investor scrutiny, this framework ensures rigorous assessment.

### Due Diligence Checklist

| Area | Key Questions & Criteria |
|------|------------------------|
| **1. Founder-Market Fit** | Domain expertise in the target industry? Prior startup experience (successful or failed — both are informative)? Complementary co-founder skills (e.g., technical + commercial)? Full-time commitment? Coachability and resilience? |
| **2. Product** | Working product vs. MVP vs. prototype — what stage? User engagement metrics (DAU/MAU, session length, feature adoption)? Technical defensibility (proprietary algorithms, unique data, architectural moat)? Product roadmap clarity? |
| **3. Market** | TAM/SAM/SOM with bottom-up derivation (not just top-down % of large market)? Market growth rate (CAGR)? Timing indicators — why now? Secular tailwinds supporting the opportunity? |
| **4. Business Model** | Unit economics (LTV/CAC ≥ 3:1, gross margins, CAC payback < 18 months)? Path to profitability articulated? Revenue model clarity (subscription, transactional, marketplace, usage-based)? Pricing power and expansion revenue potential? |
| **5. Competition** | Competitive moat — network effects, switching costs, data advantage, brand, regulatory capture? Incumbent response risk — will a large player copy or acquire? Differentiation sustainability over 3–5 years? |
| **6. Traction** | Growth rate — MoM and QoQ revenue or user growth? Customer quality — logos, contract sizes, NPS? Customer concentration — any single customer > 20% of revenue? Retention and churn metrics — cohort analysis? |
| **7. Cap Table** | Existing investors and their reputation/value-add? Dilution history — how much equity has the founding team retained? Option pool size and allocation? Pro-rata rights for existing investors? Clean structure or complex? |
| **8. Terms** | Valuation (pre-money and post-money) — justified by comparables and traction? Instrument type — SAFE vs. convertible note vs. priced round? Liquidation preferences — 1x non-participating is standard; anything else needs justification. Anti-dilution provisions — broad-based weighted average is standard. |
| **9. IP & Legal** | Patent status — filed, pending, granted? Trade secrets and proprietary processes? Regulatory requirements in target market? Pending litigation or legal disputes? Employment agreements and IP assignment clauses? |
| **10. References** | Customer references — are customers willing to vouch for the product? Former employee references — what is the working culture like? Co-investor references — have other sophisticated investors validated the opportunity? |

### Red Flags Checklist

When any of these signals appear, proceed with extreme caution or pass on the investment:

| Red Flag | Why It Matters |
|----------|---------------|
| **Customer concentration > 30% in a single customer** | Revenue fragility; one lost customer can be catastrophic |
| **Declining growth rate (deceleration)** | Market may be saturating or product-market fit may be weakening |
| **LTV/CAC < 1.5** | Business is destroying value with each new customer acquired |
| **Founder unable to articulate key metrics** | Suggests lack of analytical rigor or data-driven decision-making |
| **No technical co-founder for a tech company** | Outsourced development creates dependency, slower iteration, and higher costs |
| **Unusually complex cap table** | Prior messy rounds, too many small investors, or unfavorable terms create governance headaches |
| **Excessive burn rate relative to milestones** | Cash management discipline is critical; high burn with unclear milestones signals poor execution |
| **Founder turnover or co-founder departures** | Team instability at early stages is a strong negative signal |
| **No clear path to next funding round** | The company may run out of runway before achieving the milestones needed to raise again |
| **Pivot history without clear learning** | Pivots are fine if each one shows validated learning; serial pivots without learning suggest lack of focus |

### Investment Decision SCR Template

Use the SCR (Situation-Complication-Resolution) format for structured investment memos and decisions:

**Situation:**
- Market overview: size, growth rate, key trends, and macro tailwinds
- Startup description: what the company does, founding date, team, stage, and current traction
- Deal terms: valuation, instrument, round size, and key investors

**Complication:**
- Key risks: identify the top 3–5 risks that could cause the investment to fail (market risk, execution risk, technology risk, competitive risk, regulatory risk)
- Valuation assessment: is the price justified by traction, comparables, and growth potential?
- Portfolio fit: does this investment complement existing portfolio holdings or create concentration risk?

**Resolution:** One of three outcomes with specific rationale:
- **Invest** — state the amount, terms, and key conditions; identify what must go right
- **Don't Invest** — state the primary reason(s) clearly; note conditions under which you would reconsider
- **Invest with Conditions** — state the conditions that must be met before deploying capital (e.g., hit $X MRR, close a key hire, resolve legal issue)

**Example structure for an investment memo:**

```
SITUATION: [Company] is a Series Seed SaaS startup in [market] with $[X]K MRR
growing [Y]% MoM. Founded in [year] by [founders with relevant background].
Raising $[Z]M on a SAFE at $[V]M cap.

COMPLICATION: While growth is strong, the company faces three key risks:
(1) [Market risk], (2) [Execution risk], (3) [Valuation risk — comparable
companies at this stage have raised at lower valuations].

RESOLUTION: Invest $[amount] with the following conditions: [conditions].
Expected return scenario: [X]x in [Y] years if the company achieves [milestone].
Key monitoring metrics: MRR growth, churn, LTV/CAC, runway.
```
