## Session 1: Positional Bargaining & Fundamentals

### Core Concepts

**ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement):** The overlap between each party's reservation points where a deal is possible.

**BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement):** Your walk-away option. The stronger your BATNA, the more bargaining power you have. Always assess both your own and the other party's BATNA.

### ZOPA Calculation Example

**Salary Negotiation Scenario:**

| Element | Candidate (You) | Employer |
|---|---|---|
| BATNA / Reservation Point | Current job at $180K | Budget ceiling at $205K |
| Target | $210K | $185K |
| Anchor (opening offer) | $225K (justified with market data) | $180K |

**ZOPA = $180K to $205K** (the overlap between both parties' reservation points)

**Strategy:**
- Anchor at $225K with a precise, justified number (e.g., "$224,500 based on Levels.fyi P75 for this role in this market")
- Negotiate toward $200-210K range, making calibrated concessions
- If no ZOPA exists (your reservation point > their ceiling), expand the pie with non-salary terms: signing bonus, equity/RSUs, performance bonus, PTO, remote flexibility, professional development budget
- Remember: the party with the better BATNA has more power. If you have a competing offer at $195K, your reservation point rises and your leverage increases

**Anchoring:**
- First offers set the psychological reference point for the entire negotiation
- Precise numbers (e.g., $98,500) are more effective than round numbers ($100,000) — perceived as more informed
- **Defensive anchoring:** Ignore their anchor (don't ask them to justify), focus on their BATNA, separate information from influence, make extreme counter then propose moderation

**Concession Principles:**
- Leave lots of room for concessions
- Make concessions at the right time
- Trade concessions one at a time (code of reciprocity)
- Have a concession-making strategy

**End-Game Principles:**
- Be first to make the final commitment
- Try not to use the word "final"
- Allow for face-saving

**Use of Time:** Negotiation is not a race. Those who rush do so at their peril. "Time is money" usually applies in reverse in negotiation.

### Communication Traits of Effective Negotiators (vs. Average)
| Trait | Effective | Average |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | 63% of time | Less |
| Questions asked | 2.5× more | Fewer |
| Test & summarize | 2× more | Less |
| Irritants per hour | ⅔ fewer | More |
| Counter proposals | 50% fewer per hour | More |
| Defend/attack cycles | ⅓ as likely | More likely |
| Label their behavior | Better | Worse |
| Justifications per issue | Fewer | More |

**Communication mode preference (descending effectiveness):** In person → Video → Zoom/Teams → Telephone → Written → Email → Text. Always try to schedule at least one in-person session.

## Session 2: Integrative Negotiation, Constituents & Styles

### Beyond Positional Bargaining

**The Negotiator's Dilemma:** Maximize joint value OR maximize own value? Answer: **Do both. First maximize joint value.**

**The Most Important Word in Negotiations: INTERESTS** — What is truly important for each party.

**Three Kinds of Interests:**
1. **Substantive:** Money, property, tangible benefits
2. **Procedural:** The manner in which the dispute is dealt with
3. **Psychological/Ego:** Perception of being trusted, respected, treated fairly. "Most deals are 50% emotion."

**Four Processes Central to Integrative Negotiation:**
1. Create a free flow of information
2. Put aside assumptions about the other party
3. Understand the other negotiator's real needs and objectives
4. Search for solutions that meet goals of both parties

**Key tactics:** Use "supposals" ("Suppose we did this...") instead of positions. Be firm on primary interests, flexible on how they're met.

### Second Table / Constituent Management

Negotiators face two tables: the "first table" (direct counterparty) and the "second table" (their own constituents — HR, bosses, factions). Constituents typically expect more than can be delivered and expect tougher negotiation.

**Managing constituents:**
- Understand internal factions and their priorities
- Help your counterparty sell the deal to their second table
- Provide them with ammunition (data, framing) for internal advocacy

### Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Styles (TKI)

| Style | Assertiveness | Empathy | When to Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Avoiding** | Low | Low | Issue not worth engaging; need cooling off |
| **Accommodating** | Low | High | Relationship matters more than outcome |
| **Competing** | High | Low | True one-shot distributive deal; zero-sum |
| **Collaborating** | High | High | Relationship matters AND outcomes matter |
| **Compromising** | Medium | Medium | Time pressure; good enough solution needed |

**Advice by style:**
- **Avoiders:** Reframe "negotiation," prepare thoroughly, get lines ready, act as if negotiating for someone you care about
- **Accommodators:** Don't say "yes" too early, aim higher than resistance point, develop BATNA, bargain on behalf of someone else
- **Competitors:** Expand the pie, ask more questions, acknowledge the other party, don't haggle when you can negotiate
- **Collaborators:** Not all issues are worth negotiating; time is valuable; sometimes decide

## Session 3: Ethics, Gender & Cross-Cultural Negotiation

### Ethics in Negotiation

**Typology of deceptive tactics (ethical → unethical):**
Misrepresentation of position → Falsification → Deception → Misrepresentation to constituencies

**Key principle:** A lie is what people think it is. Misrepresentation of a material fact = lying.

**Practical safeguards:**
- Apply the "front-page test"
- Apply the Golden Rule in reverse
- Pre-plan responses to questions you don't want to answer
- Follow through on all commitments; don't break your word

**Self-protection:** Be very careful when outcome matters but relationship doesn't. Involve other relationships. Prepare thoroughly and probe carefully.

### Gender and Negotiation
- Women tend to be less likely to initiate distributive negotiation and less confident in distributive settings
- When women negotiate on behalf of someone else, the gender gap disappears
- In integrative/collaborative settings, there is no outcome difference between men and women
- Where information is plentiful, gender difference diminishes

### Cross-Cultural Negotiation
Cultural context affects: communication style, relationship expectations, pace, formality, attitudes toward conflict, decision-making process, and framing.

## Session 4: Difficult Negotiators, Multi-Party & Conflict Resolution

### Dealing with Power Disadvantage
1. First: Are they really more powerful? (How good is their BATNA, really?)
2. **Reframe:** "What will they do without me?" NOT "What will I do without them?"

### Moving Distributive Negotiators to Integrative

**Step 1: Control your own reactions — don't escalate!** Anger reduces cognitive ability.

**Step 2: Check assumptions.** They're probably not evil, stupid, or irrational. Likely: (1) you don't know how the world looks to them, or (2) this is just how they operate until they meet resistance.

**Step 3: The YES-NO-YES Framework:**
- **YES:** "This is important for me/us"
- **NO:** "I am not going to negotiate in this manner"
- **YES:** "What I propose is that we look at the issues this way"

**Step 4: De-escalate emotions:**
- Listen (it's not about who is right)
- Acknowledge their points wherever possible
- See their points as one valid point of view
- Say "Yes...and" not "But...and"
- Counter in a way that makes them think ("How could I do that?")

**Step 5: Give reasons to agree:**
- Ask for and build on their ideas
- Offer choices/options
- Help them write their victory speech
- Help them save face

**Step 6: Confront with reality (nicely):**
- "What do you think will happen if we don't agree?"
- "What do you think I will do?"
- "What will you do?"

### Three Approaches to Resolving Conflict

| Approach | Method | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Power** | Coerce, threaten, protest | Last resort |
| **Rights** | Invoke standards, contracts, norms | When clear standards exist |
| **Interests** | Understand underlying needs, reconcile | Default/preferred approach |

**Bringing rights/power negotiators back to interests:** Don't reciprocate. Don't get personal. Send mixed messages with interest-based proposals. Provide opportunity to meet. Try process interventions. Paraphrase.

## Ten Frames in Negotiation (Lewicki/Dubey)

| Frame | Description | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Substantive** | What is this negotiation really about? | Misdiagnosing the core issue |
| **Outcome** | Fixation on one particular outcome | Tunnel vision, missed value |
| **Aspiration** | Focus on satisfying multiple interests | Best for value creation |
| **Process** | Concerned with how dispute is resolved | May deprioritize outcomes |
| **Identity** | Awareness of group/cultural membership | Can distort perception |
| **Characterization** | Negative view of the other side | Creates "us vs. them" |
| **Loss-Gain** | Perceiving outcomes as losses or gains | Loss frame → higher risk tolerance (dangerous) |
| **Interests** | Focus on underlying needs | Best for integrative outcomes |
| **Rights** | Belief in being "right" or "just" | Hardest frame to shift |
| **Power** | Behavior based on power advantage | Damages relationships |

**Critical insight:** Identity + Characterization frames create "skewed vision" (Sebenius). Frames are dynamic and change during negotiation. **Reframing is often a strategic necessity.**

## Six Habits of Merely Effective Negotiators (Sebenius, HBR)

1. Neglecting BATNAs (yours and theirs)
2. Searching too hard for common ground (missing integrative opportunities)
3. "It's their problem!" (failing to understand opponent's real situation)
4. Letting positions drive out interests
5. Neglecting constituents / second table dynamics
6. Not adjusting for cross-cultural or situational context

## Perceptual Distortions & Biases

| Bias | Description |
|---|---|
| **Stereotyping** | Judging based on group membership (characterization frame) |
| **Halo Effect** | Generalizing from one attribute (tall people = better managers) |
| **Selective Perception** | Choosing info that confirms existing beliefs |
| **Projection** | Assigning your own beliefs/feelings to others |
| **Irrational Escalation** | Continuing harmful course because you took a position |
| **Mythical Fixed-Pie** | Assuming all negotiations are distributive |
| **Availability Bias** | Vivid/clear info recalled more easily |
| **Winner's Curse** | Feeling you left something on the table |

**Barriers to Persuasion:** Reluctance to give up options, fear of mistakes, social pressure, fear of losing.

## Paroz's Application Context

**Real-world negotiation analyzed in reflective paper:** Transition from Dakota LLP (data consulting) to Patek Synergy as Solution Architect.

**Frameworks applied:**
- **BATNA analysis:** Recognized weak BATNA at Dakota (instability), leveraged deep knowledge of Patek's systems as asset
- **Anchoring:** Anchored salary at $175,000 based on Dakota's $200,000 billing rate vs. $90,000 actual salary. Precise, justified anchor.
- **Framing/Reframing:** Shifted narrative from "what I'd lose without Patek" to "what Patek would lose without my expertise." Cost perspective → investment perspective.
- **Second Table management:** Helped Ms. Helen build the case to HR by providing data on cost savings, immediate productivity, and long-term strategic value. Collaborative problem-solving with internal stakeholders.
- **Creative value structuring:** Bridged salary gap with $145K base + $20K annual performance bonus + $30K long-term incentive (2-year vest) — aligning compensation with Patek's strategic objectives.
- **Authenticity & Competence challenges:** Navigated imposter syndrome when negotiating above previous pay grade by anchoring assertions in verifiable facts and past contributions.

**Key self-assessment insights:**
- Strength: Specialized knowledge, strategic framing, BATNA awareness
- Weakness: Initially limited view of alternatives; tendency to undervalue own position
- Growth: Learned to manage emotions, use interests-based approach, leverage information strategically

## When to Use This Skill

- **Salary/compensation negotiation** → Anchoring, BATNA, creative structuring, second table management
- **Stakeholder advocacy / budget asks** → Framing, reframing, interests-based approach, preparation sheet
- **Difficult conversations** → YES-NO-YES framework, de-escalation tactics, conflict resolution approaches
- **Vendor/partner negotiations** → Integrative negotiation, four central processes, constituent management
- **Cross-cultural negotiations** → Cultural context awareness, framing sensitivity, Dubey ethics framework
- **Conflict diagnosis** → Ten Frames analysis, TKI style assessment, power/rights/interests model
- **Team dynamics / internal disputes** → Conflict resolution approaches, reframing, collaborative problem-solving
- **Interview/offer negotiations** → Preparation sheet, anchoring tactics, concession strategy, value creation

## Cross-Course Connections

### Critical Thinking (NBAB 5880, Prof. Risa Mish)

| Framework | Negotiation Application |
|---|---|
| **IDEALS (7-step method)** | Use as a structured negotiation preparation protocol: Identify the problem → Define context → Enumerate options → Analyze options → List reasons → Self-correct. Maps directly to Dubey's Quick Negotiation Preparation Sheet — IDEALS ensures you have rigorously examined the problem space before sitting down at the table. |
| **10-10-10 (Decision Stakes Calibration)** | Before any negotiation, ask: How will I feel about this outcome in 10 minutes? 10 months? 10 years? This calibrates how much preparation and concession flexibility is warranted. A job offer negotiation (10-year impact) deserves far more preparation than a vendor price discussion (10-month impact). |
| **Know / Assume / Don't Know** | Sort all negotiation information into three buckets before the session. What you *know* (verified facts) anchors your position. What you *assume* (educated guesses about their BATNA, interests, constraints) should be tested with questions. What you *don't know* drives your question list — skilled negotiators ask 2.5x more questions than average negotiators precisely to convert unknowns into knowns. |

### Managerial Finance (NCCB 5050, Prof. Yaniv Grinstein)

| Framework | Negotiation Application |
|---|---|
| **Time Value of Money (TVM)** | Essential for valuing deferred compensation. A $20K signing bonus today is worth more than $20K in deferred comp paid over two years. Use PV calculations to compare offers with different timing structures. Discount rate = your personal opportunity cost of capital (what you could earn investing that money). |
| **NPV for Package Comparison** | When comparing two compensation packages with different structures (e.g., Package A: $200K salary vs. Package B: $185K + $30K equity vesting over 3 years + $15K annual bonus), convert everything to NPV using a consistent discount rate (typically 8-12% for personal financial decisions). The higher-NPV package is financially superior. |
| **IRR for Career Decisions** | Frame career moves as investments: the "cost" is any short-term compensation sacrifice, the "return" is the long-term earnings trajectory. Calculate IRR to compare staying put vs. accepting a lower-paying role with higher growth potential. |

### Transformational Leadership (NBAB 5400, Prof. Julian Barling)

| Framework | Negotiation Application |
|---|---|
| **Charismatic Leadership Tactics (CLTs)** | The 9 verbal CLTs are directly applicable to persuasive framing at the negotiation table. Use *metaphors* to reframe positions ("This isn't a cost — it's an investment in a partnership"). Use *rhetorical questions* to shift perspective ("What would it mean for your team to have this problem solved in 30 days instead of 90?"). Use *contrast* to anchor ("Compared to the $500K cost of a failed hire, this signing bonus is trivial"). Use *three-part lists* to make proposals memorable and structured. |
| **Inspirational Motivation (The 4 I's)** | In integrative negotiations, create a shared vision of what success looks like for both parties. This moves the conversation from positional bargaining to collaborative problem-solving. Frame proposals in terms of shared purpose: "We both want this partnership to succeed for the next five years — let's structure terms that make that inevitable." |
| **Individualized Consideration** | Treat your counterpart as a unique individual with specific needs, not a generic "opponent." This maps directly to Dubey's emphasis on understanding all three kinds of interests (substantive, procedural, psychological). People agree more readily when they feel genuinely understood. |

### Marketing Strategy (NBAB 6220, Prof. Monica LaBarge)

| Framework | Negotiation Application |
|---|---|
| **Value Proposition Positioning** | Your opening anchor is essentially a value proposition: "Here's what I bring, here's what it's worth." Frame your ask not as a demand but as a fair exchange of value. Use the same positioning discipline: target audience (the hiring manager/counterpart), key benefit (what problem you solve), reason to believe (evidence of past performance). |
| **Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)** | The deepest negotiation insight comes from understanding the counterpart's underlying *job to be done*, not their stated position. A hiring manager asking for a lower salary may actually need budget predictability (job = manage department costs). Offer a lower base with a performance bonus structure — you address their real job while maintaining your total comp target. JTBD thinking is the marketing equivalent of interests-based negotiation. |
| **Consumer Decision Journey** | Negotiation is not a single event but a journey with multiple touchpoints. Map the counterpart's decision process: Who else influences the decision? What information do they need at each stage? When are they most receptive? This connects directly to second-table management — understanding the full decision journey behind your counterpart's "yes." |

### Global Strategy (NBAB 5840, Prof. Michael A. Sartor)

| Framework | Negotiation Application |
|---|---|
| **Hofstede Cultural Dimensions** | Cultural dimensions directly predict negotiation styles. *High power distance* cultures expect hierarchical protocol and senior representation. *Individualist* cultures focus on personal outcomes; *collectivist* cultures prioritize group harmony and relationship preservation. *High uncertainty avoidance* cultures prefer detailed contracts and formal processes. *Long-term orientation* cultures invest more time in relationship-building before substantive negotiation. Misreading these dimensions is one of Sebenius's "six habits of merely effective negotiators." |
| **IJV (International Joint Venture) Negotiation** | Multi-party, cross-cultural negotiations require: (1) understanding each party's institutional context (regulatory, political, economic), (2) building trust across cultural distance before pushing for terms, (3) accounting for CAGE distance in valuation and risk assessment, (4) designing governance structures that manage ongoing negotiation (IJVs require continuous renegotiation, not a single deal). |
| **CAGE Distance Framework** | When negotiating across borders, use CAGE (Cultural, Administrative, Geographic, Economic distance) to anticipate friction points. Greater distance = more preparation needed, more relationship-building time, more explicit process agreements, and more flexibility on procedural interests. |

### Total Compensation Valuation

When negotiating job offers, convert all elements to a comparable annual or NPV basis to make rational comparisons.

| Compensation Element | How to Value | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Base Salary** | Face value, annualized | $200K/year — the anchor for all other calculations |
| **Equity / RSUs** | (Number of shares × current price) ÷ vesting period, adjusted for expected growth. Apply a discount of 20-40% for illiquidity and vesting risk. For public companies, use trailing 3-year CAGR as growth estimate. For pre-IPO, discount 50-70%. | 1,000 RSUs × $150/share = $150K over 4-year vest = $37.5K/year (before growth/discount adjustments) |
| **Signing Bonus** | One-time value; factor into Year 1 total comp only. If clawback clause exists (e.g., must repay if leaving within 12 months), adjust for the probability you stay. | $25K signing bonus → adds $25K to Year 1 only |
| **Performance Bonus** | Target bonus × probability of achievement. Use 80-90% if "guaranteed" / meets expectations. Use 50-60% if tied to stretch targets. Use 30-40% if company-wide metric with high variance. | $40K target bonus × 80% probability = $32K expected value |
| **PTO / Vacation** | (Annual salary ÷ 260 working days) × additional days vs. current. This is the "daily rate" method. | $200K ÷ 260 = $769/day. 5 extra days = $3,846 annual value |
| **Remote Work / Flexibility** | Commute cost savings (transit, gas, parking, wear) + time value of commute hours + flexibility premium. Conservative estimate: $5K-15K/year depending on commute distance. | 1-hour commute saved × 3 days/week × 48 weeks × $40/hour implicit value = $5,760 + $3,000 transit savings = $8,760/year |
| **Professional Development** | Face value of tuition reimbursement, conference budgets, certification support. Discount if conditional on tenure. | $10K/year tuition reimbursement with 2-year commitment = $10K/year if you plan to stay |

**Comparison Framework:**
```
Total Comp (Annual) = Base + Expected Bonus + (Equity Annual Value) + Signing Bonus (Year 1 only)
                    + PTO Delta Value + Remote Work Value + Development Budget

NPV (3-Year) = Sum of [Total Comp Year t / (1 + r)^t] for t = 1, 2, 3
where r = your personal discount rate (typically 8-12%)
```

**Pro tip:** Build a simple spreadsheet comparing offers side by side on all dimensions. Quantifying every element removes emotion from the decision and gives you objective ammunition at the negotiation table. This connects back to Managerial Finance (NPV comparison) and Critical Thinking (Know/Assume/Don't Know — separate verified comp data from assumptions about bonus probability).
