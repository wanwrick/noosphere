## Core Frameworks

### 1. IS / IT / IM Framework

The foundational model for understanding digital business architecture:

```
Digital Business
       |
  Information Management (IM) — the management layer
       |
  Information Technology (IT) — the technology layer
       |
  Information System (IS) — the operational layer
```

**Information System (IS)** consists of five components:

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Hardware** | Physical computing devices, servers, networks, IoT sensors |
| **Software** | Applications, platforms, operating systems, algorithms |
| **Data** | Structured and unstructured information assets |
| **Procedures** | Business processes, workflows, rules, policies |
| **People** | Users, developers, managers, stakeholders |

**Information Technology (IT)** is the technology layer — the hardware, software, and networking infrastructure that enables the information system.

**Information Management (IM)** is the management layer — the governance, strategy, and decision-making that directs how IT and IS are deployed to serve business objectives.

**Digital Business** sits on top of all three layers, representing the strategic transformation of business models, customer engagement, and operations through digital technologies.

**Key insight:** Technology alone (IT) does not create value. Value emerges from the alignment of technology (IT) with systems (IS) under effective management (IM) to enable digital business outcomes.

### 2. Three Types of Digital Strategies

Organizations pursue digital transformation along three strategic paths:

| Strategy Type | Focus | Goal | Example |
|--------------|-------|------|---------|
| **Customer Engagement** | Front-end digital experiences | Deepen customer relationships, personalize interactions, expand reach | Prada's omnichannel luxury experience |
| **Digital Solutions** | New digitally-enabled products and services | Create new value propositions, monetize data, build platforms | Kroger's data analytics and personalization |
| **Operational Excellence** | Back-end process digitalization | Reduce costs, increase speed, improve quality, automate operations | ANZ's agile transformation |

**Key insight:** These strategies are not mutually exclusive but organizations typically lead with one dominant type based on their competitive context.

### 3. Digital Definitions: Digitization, Digitalization, Digital Transformation

Three distinct levels of digital change, often conflated but strategically different:

```
Digitization → Digitalization → Digital Transformation
(Data)         (Processes)       (Strategy + Structure + Culture)
```

| Term | Definition | Scope of Change | Example |
|------|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Digitization** | Converting analog information into digital format | Data layer only | Scanning paper records into PDFs, converting film to digital |
| **Digitalization** | Using digital technologies to change manual business processes | Process layer | Moving from in-store purchasing to e-commerce, automating invoice processing |
| **Digital Transformation** | Fundamental change in strategy, organizational structure, and culture enabled by digital technologies | Enterprise-wide | GE's (attempted) pivot to an industrial IoT platform company |

**Key insight:** Most organizations mistake digitization or digitalization for digital transformation. True digital transformation requires changes to strategy, structure, AND culture — not just technology adoption.

### 4. Three Classic Questions and Three Modern Questions

#### Three Classic Questions (Information-Age Foundation)
1. **Information:** What information does the business need to operate and compete?
2. **Technology:** What technology is required to collect, store, process, and distribute that information?
3. **Policies:** What governance policies ensure the information and technology are managed effectively, securely, and ethically?

#### Three Modern Questions (Digital-Age Extension)
1. **Processes:** How should digital technologies transform existing business processes?
2. **Products/Services:** How can digital technologies create new products, services, or value propositions?
3. **New Businesses:** How can digital technologies enable entirely new business models or market opportunities?

**Key insight:** The classic questions focus on managing what exists; the modern questions focus on creating what is new. Effective digital strategy addresses both simultaneously.

### 5. Economics of Information: Richness vs. Reach

A foundational concept from the pre-digital economy:

**Traditional trade-off:**
- **Richness:** Depth, detail, customization, and bandwidth of information (e.g., face-to-face sales consultation)
- **Reach:** Number of people who can access the information (e.g., mass-market advertising)

In the analog economy, richness and reach were inversely related — you could have one or the other, but not both.

**Digital economy disruption:** Digital technologies collapse the richness-reach trade-off. Organizations can now deliver rich, personalized information to massive audiences simultaneously.

| Dimension | Analog Era | Digital Era |
|-----------|-----------|------------|
| **Richness** | Limited by physical channels, personal interaction | Personalized at scale through AI, data analytics |
| **Reach** | Limited by distribution infrastructure | Global through digital platforms, near-zero marginal cost |
| **Trade-off** | Choose one | Both achievable simultaneously |

**Examples:** Netflix delivers personalized recommendations (richness) to 200M+ subscribers (reach). Kroger delivers personalized offers through analytics at massive scale.

### 6. Framework 1: The Marketing Practitioner Lens (David Rodgers)

Core thesis: "Digital transformation is about strategy, not technology."

**Key principles:**
- Technology is the enabler, not the objective
- Successful digital transformation starts with strategic clarity about customer value, competitive positioning, and business model evolution
- Organizations that lead with technology initiatives (without strategic grounding) risk the GE trap — massive investment with unclear strategic purpose
- The question is not "What technology should we adopt?" but "What strategic problem are we solving, and can digital technologies help?"

**Application:** This framework serves as a diagnostic filter — when evaluating any digital initiative, first ask whether it is strategically motivated or technology-motivated. Technology-motivated initiatives without clear strategic linkage carry high failure risk.

## Case Study Memo Framework

The case study memo follows a strict 2-page format. This is a core methodology of the course and must be followed precisely.

### Memo Structure

```
MEMORANDUM

TO:     [Decision-maker in the case]
FROM:   [Team members]
SUBJECT: [Concise statement of the decision/recommendation]
DATE:   [Date]
```

### Section 1: Five Key Issues

Identify and briefly describe the five most critical issues facing the organization. These should be concise, specific, and directly relevant to the case situation.

### Section 2: Problem Statement / Decision Statement

A single, clear statement that frames the central problem or decision the organization faces. This is the anchor for the entire memo — everything else flows from this.

### Section 3: Three Alternatives

Present three distinct, viable courses of action. Each alternative should be:
- Realistic and actionable
- Meaningfully different from the others (not variations on the same theme)
- Directly responsive to the problem statement

### Section 4: Three Evaluation Criteria

Define three criteria against which the alternatives will be evaluated. Criteria should be:
- Relevant to the strategic context of the case
- Measurable or at least assessable
- Weighted or prioritized if appropriate

### Section 5: Evaluation and Recommendation

Evaluate each alternative against each criterion. This is typically presented as a matrix:

| | Criterion 1 | Criterion 2 | Criterion 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alternative 1** | Assessment | Assessment | Assessment |
| **Alternative 2** | Assessment | Assessment | Assessment |
| **Alternative 3** | Assessment | Assessment | Assessment |

State which alternative is recommended and why.

### Section 6: Three Action Items

Three specific, concrete implementation steps for the recommended alternative. Actions should include:
- Who is responsible
- What specifically needs to happen
- Timeline or sequencing

### Format Requirements

- **Strict 2-page limit** — no exceptions
- Professional memorandum format
- Concise, direct language — no filler
- Evidence-based reasoning drawn from case facts
- Structured sections as outlined above

## Digital Transformation Metrics & Investment Analysis

### Digital Transformation KPIs

Quantitative metrics for tracking digital transformation progress. These KPIs map to the three digital strategy types (Customer Engagement, Digital Solutions, Operational Excellence) and provide measurable targets for transformation initiatives.

| KPI | Definition | Formula / Measurement | Benchmark Range | Strategy Type |
|-----|-----------|----------------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Digital Revenue %** | Percentage of total revenue generated through digital channels | `Digital Channel Revenue / Total Revenue × 100` | 20-60% (varies by industry; retail 30-50%, banking 40-70%, B2B 15-30%) | Customer Engagement, Digital Solutions |
| **Process Automation Rate** | Percentage of repeatable business processes fully automated | `Automated Processes / Total Automatable Processes × 100` | 30-50% (laggards <15%, leaders >60%) | Operational Excellence |
| **Digital Customer Adoption Rate** | Percentage of customers actively using digital services | `Customers Using Digital Channels / Total Active Customers × 100` | 50-80% (banking 60-85%, insurance 30-50%, retail 40-70%) | Customer Engagement |
| **Employee Digital Literacy Score** | Composite measure of workforce digital skills (survey-based, 1-100) | Weighted average of digital skills assessment across role categories | 60-75 baseline; target 80+ for digitally mature orgs | All three types |
| **System Integration Index** | Percentage of enterprise systems connected on a unified platform | `Integrated Systems / Total Enterprise Systems × 100` | 40-70% (target 80%+ for Level 4-5 maturity) | Operational Excellence |
| **Time-to-Market (Digital Features)** | Average elapsed time from concept approval to production deployment | Calendar days from business case approval to GA release | 30-90 days (agile orgs); 6-18 months (traditional) | Digital Solutions |
| **Digital Channel NPS** | Net Promoter Score specifically for digital service channels | `% Promoters (9-10) − % Detractors (0-6)` on digital experience survey | 30-50 (good); 50-70 (excellent); >70 (world-class) | Customer Engagement |

**Dashboard Construction Guidance:**
- Track KPIs monthly or quarterly depending on measurement cadence
- Set baselines before transformation begins (Year 0 measurement)
- Define 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year targets for each KPI
- Weight KPIs by strategic priority (e.g., if leading with Operational Excellence, weight Process Automation Rate and System Integration Index more heavily)

### Technology Investment ROI Framework

#### NPV Formula for Technology Investments

```
NPV = Σ [t=0 to n] (Bt − Ct) / (1 + r)^t

Where:
  Bt = Benefits in year t (efficiency gains + revenue uplift + risk reduction)
  Ct = Costs in year t (implementation + ongoing + opportunity costs)
  r  = Discount rate (typically WACC; use 8-12% for most enterprises)
  n  = Project horizon (typically 3-5 years for digital initiatives)
```

**Payback Period:** `Year where Cumulative Discounted Cash Flow ≥ 0`

**IRR:** Solve for r where NPV = 0. For digital initiatives, target IRR > WACC + risk premium (typically IRR > 15-20%).

#### Typical ROI Ranges by Digital Initiative Type

| Initiative Type | Examples | Typical ROI | Payback Period | Risk Profile | Key Value Drivers |
|----------------|----------|-------------|---------------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Customer-Facing Digital** | Mobile app, e-commerce platform, omnichannel CX, self-service portals | 15-25% | 18-24 months | Medium-High (market adoption uncertainty) | Revenue uplift 5-15%, customer retention +10-20%, acquisition cost reduction 15-25% |
| **Process Automation** | RPA, workflow automation, document processing, automated testing | 25-40% | 12-18 months | Low-Medium (well-defined scope, measurable baseline) | Labor cost reduction 20-40%, error rate reduction 50-80%, throughput increase 30-60% |
| **Data & Analytics Platform** | Data warehouse/lakehouse, BI dashboards, ML/AI models, data governance | 20-30% | 18-30 months | Medium (value realization depends on organizational adoption) | Decision speed +30-50%, forecast accuracy +15-25%, new revenue from data monetization 5-10% |

### Digital Maturity Assessment Rubric

Score each dimension 1-5. Compute an unweighted average for overall maturity, or apply weights aligned to strategic priorities.

| Level | Label | Strategy | Organization | Technology | Data | Culture | Description |
|-------|-------|----------|-------------|------------|------|---------|-------------|
| **1** | **Ad Hoc** | No digital strategy; reactive technology adoption | No digital roles; IT siloed from business | Legacy systems, minimal integration, manual processes | Data scattered across spreadsheets; no governance | Resistance to change; digital seen as IT's problem | Organization has not begun intentional digital transformation |
| **2** | **Emerging** | Pilot digital projects; no enterprise-wide vision | Digital champion(s) but no formal structure | Some cloud adoption; isolated digital tools | Basic reporting; data in departmental silos | Curiosity about digital; pockets of experimentation | Individual initiatives exist but are not coordinated or integrated |
| **3** | **Defined** | Digital strategy documented and communicated | Dedicated digital team or CDO appointed | Cloud-first policy; some system integration; APIs emerging | Central data repository; basic analytics; initial governance | Digital literacy training in progress; growing buy-in | Organization has a plan and is executing, but inconsistently across units |
| **4** | **Managed** | Digital KPIs tracked; strategy tied to business metrics | Cross-functional digital teams; agile practices adopted | Integrated platforms; automated CI/CD; modern architecture | Enterprise data platform; advanced analytics & ML in use; formal governance | Digital-first mindset in most functions; leaders model digital behaviors | Metrics-driven transformation with organization-wide execution |
| **5** | **Optimized** | Digital and business strategy fully fused; continuous reinvention | Digital embedded in every function; no separate "digital team" needed | Composable architecture; real-time data; AI-augmented operations | Data as a strategic asset; self-service analytics; data monetization | Continuous learning culture; innovation is everyone's responsibility | Organization operates as a digital-native entity with continuous innovation |

**Scoring Guidance:**
- Assess each of the 5 dimensions (Strategy, Organization, Technology, Data, Culture) independently
- Overall Score = Average of 5 dimension scores
- Target: Move up 1 level per 12-18 months of focused transformation
- Most enterprises begin at Level 2-3; Level 5 represents digital-native leaders (e.g., Amazon, Netflix)
- Use the assessment at transformation kickoff (baseline) and then annually to track progress

## Build vs. Buy Decision Framework

A structured framework for evaluating whether to build custom solutions or buy/license existing technology. This connects to Session 3 (Generative AI strategy — build vs. buy decisions) and Operations Management (make vs. buy analysis).

### Evaluation Criteria

| Criterion | Weight (adjust per context) | Build (In-House) | Buy (Vendor/SaaS) | Key Questions |
|-----------|----|-------------------|-------------------|---------------|
| **Cost (3-Year TCO)** | 20-25% | Higher upfront (development), lower ongoing if maintained well | Lower upfront (subscription), but cumulative licensing can exceed build cost at scale | What is the 3-year TCO for each option? At what scale does build become cheaper? |
| **Time-to-Value** | 15-20% | 6-18 months (design + build + test + deploy) | 1-6 months (configure + integrate + deploy) | How urgent is the business need? Can we afford to wait for a custom build? |
| **Customization Fit** | 15-20% | Unlimited — built to exact specifications | Limited to vendor's configurability; may require workarounds for unique needs | How unique are our requirements? Does any vendor cover 80%+ of our needs? |
| **Vendor Lock-in Risk** | 10-15% | No vendor dependency (but risk of key-person dependency) | High if proprietary; moderate if standards-based; evaluate data portability and exit clauses | Can we extract our data? What are switching costs? Are APIs open-standard? |
| **Maintenance Burden** | 10-15% | Full responsibility: bug fixes, upgrades, security patches, scaling | Vendor handles core maintenance; buyer manages configuration, integrations | Do we have the team to maintain long-term? What is the vendor's SLA and track record? |
| **Regulatory Compliance** | 10-15% | Full control over data residency, audit trails, security architecture | Dependent on vendor's compliance posture; must verify certifications (SOC 2, GDPR, etc.) | What are our regulatory requirements? Does the vendor meet them today? |
| **Integration Complexity** | 5-10% | Built to integrate with existing stack; may require custom connectors | Pre-built integrations for common platforms; may lack connectors for legacy systems | How many systems must this integrate with? Are APIs available on both sides? |

### Build vs. Buy Heuristics

**Favor BUILD when:**
- The capability is a core competitive differentiator (VRIN resource — see Business Strategy)
- Requirements are highly unique and no vendor covers >60% of needs
- Regulatory requirements demand full control over data and architecture
- Long-term TCO favors build at your scale (typically >500 users or high transaction volume)
- You have strong internal engineering talent and capacity
- Integration with proprietary legacy systems is critical and complex

**Favor BUY when:**
- The capability is a commodity or industry-standard function (not a differentiator)
- Speed-to-market is critical and a proven solution exists
- Vendor covers >80% of requirements with configurable options
- Internal team lacks capacity or specialized expertise to build and maintain
- The vendor ecosystem offers pre-built integrations with your tech stack
- Total cost of internal development + maintenance exceeds 3-year licensing cost

**Consider HYBRID (buy platform, build custom layer) when:**
- Core platform is commodity but differentiation comes from customization on top
- A vendor provides 60-80% of needs, and remaining 20-40% can be built as extensions
- Regulatory requirements can be met by vendor platform with custom compliance layer
- You need fast time-to-value (buy) but plan to migrate to custom (build) over time

**Connection to Course Material:**
- GE case (Session 2): GE chose to build Predix as a custom industrial IoT platform when buying or partnering may have been more effective — illustrates the danger of defaulting to "build" without rigorous evaluation
- Generative AI (Session 3): "How to Capitalize on Generative AI" article directly addresses build vs. buy for AI capabilities — most organizations should buy/partner for foundation models and build custom applications on top
- ANZ case (Session 4): ANZ's agile transformation involved both buying tools (Jira, Confluence) and building custom agile practices — a hybrid approach
