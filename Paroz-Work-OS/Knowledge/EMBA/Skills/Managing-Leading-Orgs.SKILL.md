## Course Philosophy

**Core thesis:** Leadership is a choice, not a title. The course examines how individuals decide to lead, take accountability, overcome obstacles, and create impact — drawing from social psychology, organizational psychology, neuroscience, behavioral economics, and management.

## Session-by-Session Framework Map

### Session 1: Building a Team
**Key Concepts:**
- **Intellectual Humility:** The degree to which people recognize their beliefs might be wrong. 82% of people think they're usually the one who is right (Leary et al., 2017). Scale items: questioning own opinions, recognizing value in different views, openness to changing opinions when facing conflicting evidence.
- **80/20 Rule of Team Communication:** In most collaborative teams, 80% of talking is done by 20% of people. Effective leaders get all data on the table.
- **Building Emotional Intelligence of Groups (Druskat & Wolff, HBR):** Group EI is not about individual emotional intelligence — it's about group-level norms that build trust, group identity, and group efficacy. Three levels: individual members, the group itself, and cross-boundary relationships.
- **After Action Review (AAR):** Post-exercise debrief: What did we do well? What could we improve? What lessons apply going forward?

**Case: Army Crew Team**
- Varsity team loses to JV despite superior individual talent
- Root cause analysis: ego, lack of cohesion, poor team norms, coach's failure to address dynamics early
- Lesson: Individual talent ≠ team performance. Process and culture matter more.

**Case Analysis Framework (4 Steps):**
1. Identify issues, problems, and opportunities
2. Analyze root causes (why, not what)
3. Develop and evaluate alternative solutions
4. Make recommendations for action

### Session 2: Leading with Purpose
**Key Concepts:**
- **The Authenticity Paradox (Ibarra, HBR 2015):** True leadership growth often requires adopting behaviors that initially feel inauthentic. Leaders must experiment with new approaches rather than clinging rigidly to a fixed "authentic self." Adaptive authenticity > rigid authenticity.
- **From Purpose to Impact (Craig & Snook, HBR):** Purpose is not a destination — it's a practice. Leaders must translate personal purpose into concrete leadership behaviors and organizational impact.
- **Earliest Leadership Narrative:** Reflective exercise identifying formative leadership moments and connecting them to core values.

**Case: Carla Ann Harris at Morgan Stanley**
- Woman of color navigating male-dominated corporate finance
- Balanced personal integrity with strategic adaptation
- Key tensions: authenticity vs. organizational conformity; individual style vs. institutional expectations
- Lesson: Effective leaders adapt their style without abandoning core values. High self-monitoring is a strength, not a compromise.

---

### Session 3: Tools of Courageous Action
**Key Concepts:**
- **Courage as a Skill (Reardon, HBR 2007):** Courage is not an innate trait — it's a learnable, practicable skill.
- **Courage Quotient = Willingness to Act / Fear**

**Raising Willingness to Act:**
1. **Connect to Core Values:** Values motivate action. Ask: "Which of my core values is honored by taking this action?"
2. **Ripple Effect Analysis:** Who benefits directly? Who benefits indirectly? Who feels the ripples in the future?
3. **Hidden Costs of Inaction (When YES = NO):** When you say YES to not acting, you also say YES to: comfort, avoiding conflict, lowering standards, never knowing potential. You say NO to: getting what you want, living up to values, respecting yourself.
4. **Strategic Timing:** Is this critical now? Do I have credibility? Have I gathered allies? Real emergencies are rare — take time to prepare.

**Managing Fear:**
1. **See it:** Acknowledge and label the fear. Physical, emotional, cognitive, behavioral signals.
2. **Own it:** Accept that fear is natural and universal.
3. **Use it:** Channel fear into preparation and strategic action.

**Mindset Framework:**
- Fixed vs. Growth mindset (Dweck)
- Mindsets are malleable — can shift from "stressful/debilitating" to "learning/enhancing"

**Life Journey Line:** Reflective exercise mapping critical life events, identifying values honored or betrayed, and extracting leadership lessons.

### Session 4: The Choice to Lead
**Key Concepts:**
- **SPLIT Framework for Global Teams (Neeley, HBR 2015):** Structure, Process, Language, Identity, Technology — five dimensions for reducing social distance in geographically distributed teams.
- **Building a Global Team:** Cross-cultural leadership requires understanding cultural context, establishing shared identity, and creating processes that bridge language and geographic barriers.

**Case: (Re)Building a Global Team — Tariq Khan at Tek**
- Inherited a dysfunctional, culturally diverse global team
- Root causes: cultural fragmentation, leadership vacuum, lack of shared identity, geographic distance
- Analysis approach: diagnose root causes → develop culturally sensitive action plan → decide whether to accept the role
- Lesson: Global team turnarounds require building emotional capacity, establishing group norms, and leveraging formal authority to create trust.

**Cross-Case Synthesis (Carla × Tariq):**
- Both face high-stakes decisions with career implications
- Carla: stable corporate setting, diversity initiatives; Tariq: crisis management, global diversity
- Shared lesson: adaptive authenticity + strategic risk-taking + trust-building

### Session 5: Navigating the Organization
**Key Concepts:**
- **Human Capital vs. Social Capital:**
  - Human Capital = How you stand out (knowledge, skills, experience, judgment, character, effort)
  - Social Capital = How you are connected (social ties, trust level, benefits from ties)
  - **Social Capital allows you to leverage your Human Capital**

- **Network Assessment:** Evaluating breadth, density, and dynamism of professional relationships.
- **BCDs of Effective Networking:** Breadth (diversity), Connections (bridging boundaries), Dynamic (future-oriented)
- **Strong vs. Weak Ties:** Weak ties provide novel information and bridge structural holes; strong ties provide trust and deep collaboration.
- **Dormant Ties:** Reconnecting with dormant professional relationships can be more valuable than building new ones — shared history + new perspectives.

- **Diversity Dividend:** Network diversity improves VC financial performance, CEO firm value, decision-making quality, innovation, and breakthrough discoveries. Mechanisms: higher quality patents, ability to identify opportunities across industries/countries.

- **Gratitude as Social Glue:** Two parts — affirmation of goodness + recognizing external sources. Strengthens recall of tailwinds over headwinds. Exercises: gratitude letter, daily affirmation, mindfulness walks, proactive thank-yous.
- **Paving the Way:** Reflective exercise identifying someone who invested in your career — mentorship, advocacy, introductions.

---

### Session 6: Leading Transformational Change
**Key Concepts:**
- **The Focused Leader (Goleman, HBR):** Three types of focus: inner focus (self-awareness), other focus (empathy), outer focus (systems thinking). Effective leaders balance all three.
- **Leadership Style Questionnaire (Mannix):** Self-assessment tool for identifying dominant leadership patterns.
- **Transformational vs. Transactional Leadership:** Transformational leaders inspire change through vision, intellectual stimulation, individualized consideration, and idealized influence.

**Case: Amir Dan Rubin — Success from the Beginning (Stanford Health Care)**
- Vision-driven transformation of healthcare organization (pre-COVID)
- Where leaders spend their time signals priorities — Rubin's time allocation as leadership communication
- Obstacles to change and strategies for addressing resistance
- Lesson: Effective change agents are deliberate about time, create inclusive vision, and model desired behaviors.

---

### Session 7: Becoming a Coaching Leader
**Key Concepts:**
- **Active Listening:** Being fully present, curious, and open in conversation (Celeste Headlee, TED Talk)
- **Powerful Questions:** Open-ended questions that unlock insight rather than confirm assumptions
- **Presence:** The skill of being mentally and emotionally engaged, especially difficult in virtual settings
- **Coaching vs. Directing:** Shifting from telling people what to do to asking questions that help them discover solutions themselves

## Congruence Model (Organizational Diagnosis)

**Source:** Nadler & Tushman (1980), taught by Prof. Beta Mannix in Managing & Leading Organizations. Also used as the root-cause analysis engine in Step 3 (Determine Root Causes) of the Context + IDEALS method — see **Critical-Thinking.SKILL.md** for that cross-reference.

**Core idea:** Organizations are systems of interdependent elements. Performance problems arise not from any single element in isolation but from **misalignment (lack of fit) between elements**. Diagnosis requires examining each element and every pairwise relationship.

### The Four Elements

| Element | Definition | Diagnostic Questions |
|---------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Task** | The core work the organization must perform — workflows, processes, interdependencies, knowledge/skill requirements, intrinsic rewards of the work itself | What is the actual work being done? What workflows and interdependencies exist? What skills does the work require? How inherently motivating or routine is the work? |
| **People** | The individuals who do the work — their skills, knowledge, preferences, expectations, demographics, and needs | Who does the work? What competencies do they bring? What are their expectations and career aspirations? Are there demographic or generational dynamics? What motivates them? |
| **Formal Organization** | The explicit structures, systems, policies, procedures, reward mechanisms, reporting relationships, and resource allocation processes | What is the organizational structure? How are decisions made? What do reward systems incentivize? Are policies aligned with strategic goals? How are resources allocated? |
| **Informal Organization** | The implicit culture, norms, unwritten rules, power dynamics, social networks, and emergent patterns of behavior | What is the actual culture (vs. espoused values)? Who holds informal power? What are the unwritten rules? How do people actually communicate? What behaviors are rewarded or punished informally? |

### Pairwise Fit Analysis (6 Pairs)

The diagnostic power of the model comes from examining **alignment between every pair** of elements. Misalignment in any pair creates organizational friction, inefficiency, or dysfunction.

| Pair | Fit Question | Example of Misalignment |
|------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Task ↔ People** | Do people have the skills, knowledge, and motivation the work requires? | Highly complex analytical work assigned to team members without quantitative training |
| **Task ↔ Formal Organization** | Do structures, systems, and rewards support the work that needs to be done? | Cross-functional project work in a rigid silo-based reporting structure with unit-level KPIs |
| **Task ↔ Informal Organization** | Does the culture support the way the work needs to be performed? | Innovation-dependent work in a risk-averse, blame-heavy culture |
| **People ↔ Formal Organization** | Do formal structures meet the needs and expectations of the people? | High-autonomy professionals in a micro-managed, approval-heavy hierarchy |
| **People ↔ Informal Organization** | Does the culture match what the people need and expect? | New diverse hires in an exclusionary old-guard network culture |
| **Formal ↔ Informal Organization** | Do the explicit systems and the actual culture reinforce each other? | Formal policy says "collaboration" but informal rewards go to individual stars |

### How to Conduct a Congruence Diagnosis

1. **Map each element** — Describe the current state of Task, People, Formal, and Informal Organization with evidence
2. **Assess each pair** — For all 6 pairs, evaluate the degree of fit (strong fit, moderate fit, poor fit)
3. **Identify misalignments** — The most severe misalignments are the root causes of organizational problems
4. **Prioritize** — Rank misalignments by impact severity and tractability
5. **Design interventions** — Target specific pairs to bring into alignment; recognize that changing one element ripples through all pairs

### Application Contexts

**Organizational Change:**
- Use as the diagnostic step *before* designing any change initiative
- Identify which elements are in alignment with the desired future state and which must shift
- Predict resistance by identifying where proposed changes create new misalignments (e.g., changing formal structure without addressing informal power dynamics)

**Post-Merger Integration:**
- Map each merging organization's four elements independently
- Identify clashes across all four elements (especially Informal ↔ Informal culture clashes)
- Prioritize integration of Formal Organization (systems, processes, reporting) while being deliberate about Informal Organization (culture cannot be mandated — it must be actively built)
- Watch for Task ↔ People misalignment when roles are restructured post-merger

**Team Dysfunction Diagnosis:**
- Apply at team level: What is the team's Task? Who are the People? What Formal structures govern the team? What Informal norms have emerged?
- Connects to Session 1 (Army Crew Team): the varsity crew had strong People (talent) but poor Informal Organization (ego, lack of cohesion) and weak Formal Organization (coach's failure to set norms) — Task ↔ Informal and People ↔ Informal misalignment
- Connects to Session 4 (Tariq Khan at Tek): global team with Task ↔ People misalignment (cultural fragmentation reduced effective skill deployment) and broken Informal Organization

## Change Management Framework

**Source:** Kotter (1996) "Leading Change," reinforced across Sessions 6-7 (Transformational Leadership, Amir Dan Rubin case) and integrated with the Congruence Model for organizational transitions.

### Kotter's 8-Step Change Process

| Step | Action | Key Questions | Common Failure Mode |
|------|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| **1. Create Urgency** | Establish that the status quo is more dangerous than the unknown | What burning platform or compelling opportunity justifies change? What data makes the case irrefutable? | Complacency — people don't see the need |
| **2. Build a Guiding Coalition** | Assemble a group with enough power, credibility, and expertise to lead the change | Who has formal authority? Informal influence? Technical expertise? Who is a trusted voice? | Insufficient coalition — relying on a single champion |
| **3. Form a Strategic Vision** | Create a clear picture of the future that is easy to communicate and appeals to stakeholders | Can you describe the future state in under 5 minutes? Does it connect to people's values and aspirations? | Vague or uninspiring vision that fails to motivate |
| **4. Enlist Volunteers** | Communicate the vision broadly and repeatedly so people choose to join | Are you using multiple channels? Are leaders modeling the change? Have you addressed "What's in it for me?" | Under-communicating by a factor of 10x |
| **5. Enable Action by Removing Barriers** | Eliminate structural obstacles, misaligned systems, and resistant gatekeepers | What formal structures block the change? (Use Congruence Model: Formal ↔ Task misalignment) What skills gaps exist? (People ↔ Task) | Leaving obstacles in place while demanding new behavior |
| **6. Generate Short-Term Wins** | Plan and create visible, unambiguous improvements early | What quick wins can you deliver in 30-90 days? How will you publicize them? | Leaving short-term results to chance |
| **7. Sustain Acceleration** | Use the credibility from wins to drive deeper change — tackle bigger problems, harder systems | Are you consolidating gains before pushing further? Are you promoting and developing people who embody the change? | Declaring victory too soon |
| **8. Institute Change** | Anchor new behaviors in the culture — make it "how we do things here" | Have formal systems (rewards, hiring, processes) been updated? Does the informal culture reinforce the change? | Failing to anchor changes in Informal Organization |

### Integration with the Congruence Model

Kotter's 8 steps and the Congruence Model are complementary:

- **Congruence Model = Diagnosis** (Where are we now? What is misaligned?)
- **Kotter's 8 Steps = Execution** (How do we move from current state to desired state?)

| Kotter Step | Congruence Model Connection |
|-------------|---------------------------|
| Steps 1-3 (Urgency, Coalition, Vision) | Informed by the Congruence diagnosis — urgency comes from identified misalignments, coalition must include people who can shift each element |
| Step 5 (Remove Barriers) | Directly addresses Formal Organization misalignments — restructure, realign incentives, update policies |
| Step 8 (Institute Change) | Targets Informal Organization — culture change is the hardest and last element to shift; requires sustained reinforcement |

### Application Contexts

**Organizational Restructuring:**
1. Diagnose current state using Congruence Model (all 4 elements + 6 pairs)
2. Define desired future state for each element
3. Apply Kotter's 8 steps to close the gap, prioritizing the most severe misalignments
4. Monitor for new misalignments created by the change itself

**Post-Merger Integration:**
1. Conduct parallel Congruence diagnoses for both organizations
2. Create urgency (Step 1) around integration risks identified by the diagnosis
3. Build a coalition (Step 2) that includes influential leaders from both sides
4. Remove barriers (Step 5) by harmonizing Formal Organization (systems, processes, reporting)
5. Institute change (Step 8) by deliberately building shared Informal Organization (culture, norms)

**Team Turnarounds (connects to Session 4, Tariq Khan case):**
1. Diagnose team dysfunction using Congruence Model at team level
2. Create urgency by making the cost of dysfunction visible
3. Build coalition of willing team members
4. Generate quick wins to build momentum and trust
5. Institute new norms to shift Informal Organization

**Connection to Session 6 (Amir Dan Rubin / Stanford Health Care):**
- Rubin's transformation exemplifies Kotter's model: created urgency (healthcare quality gaps), built coalition (senior leaders), formed vision (patient-centered care), enabled action (restructured processes), generated wins (measurable quality improvements), and institutionalized change (new culture of continuous improvement)
- His deliberate time allocation demonstrated Step 4 (Enlist Volunteers) — leaders signal priorities through where they spend time
