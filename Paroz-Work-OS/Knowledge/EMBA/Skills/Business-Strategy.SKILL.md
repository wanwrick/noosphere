## MODULE 1: Industry Profit Potential

### 1.1 PEST Analysis (PESTLE)

**Purpose**: Force a broad, big-picture view of what is happening in an industry. Don't worry about which letter applies — the point is to open your eyes.

| Factor | Examples |
|--------|----------|
| **Political** | Political stability, trade pacts, tariffs, business regulations, antitrust |
| **Economic** | Macro issues: interest rates, growth rates, unemployment |
| **Social** | Demographics, attitudes toward inequality or social issues |
| **Technological** | Innovations and adoption (big and small) |
| **Legal** | Regulations, IP law, compliance requirements |
| **Environmental** | Sustainability pressures (e.g., Gucci stopping fur use) |

**How to perform PEST**:
1. Take a broad view — use PEST(LE) as a spur, not a constraint
2. Focus especially on **changes or anticipated changes**
3. Ask what changes mean in terms of **opportunities or threats**
4. Brainstorm **strategies** to cope with threats/exploit opportunities

**Key examples**: Netflix DVD → streaming (tech change enabling new distribution), cannabis industry (political uncertainty), Coke/Pepsi (social shift away from sugar)

### 1.2 Five Forces Analysis (Porter's Industry Analysis)

**Purpose**: Understand opportunities and challenges in a market — not just today but in the future. Identify which forces are most problematic and which you can do something about.

> Critical insight: The power of a five forces analysis comes from understanding the **underlying economic forces** — not from just writing firm names in five boxes.

#### Force 1: Entry Threat

**Big-picture considerations**:
- Think about who **could** enter AND who **wants** to enter
- High costs alone don't prevent entry if rewards are large enough (capital markets are vibrant)
- Consider non-monetary barriers: distribution access, expertise, IP
- Speed of entry matters — sometimes firms enter by acquisition
- **Contestability**: if entry is quick/easy, even few-firm industries may not earn high profits

**Factors making entry LESS of a threat**:
- Probable and fierce competitive response (reputation for aggression, ability to retaliate)
- Large minimum efficient scale (MES) relative to market size
- Significant switching costs
- Network effects (especially in tech markets)
- Difficulty accessing key inputs, distribution channels, or resources
- Informational problems (hard for entrants to signal quality)
- Government regulations
- Experience/learning curves

#### Force 2: Internal Rivalry

**Key question**: "What is competition like in this industry?" (prices, advertising, innovation, shelf space)

**History matters**: Are players "friendly" or "collusive"? Have they found ways to coexist?

**Factors making rivalry fiercer**:
- Overcapacity (especially with high fixed costs and MES)
- Lack of differentiation
- Large and infrequent orders
- Lack of industry growth
- Low switching costs or network effects

#### Force 3: Buyer and Supplier Power

**Key principles**:
- Upstream/downstream form separate markets — factors making internal rivalry strong in those markets lessen their power
- Distinct buyer/supplier groups may deserve separate analyses (e.g., Coke selling to fountain outlets vs. grocery stores vs. end consumers)
- Natural limits: can't pay suppliers less than average costs long-term
- Firms can influence supplier costs (e.g., Walmart working with suppliers; Apple lending to flash memory suppliers)

**Factors making buyer/supplier power LESS problematic**:
- Substitute inputs available
- Suppliers less concentrated than industry buyers
- Vertical integration is possible and credible
- Low switching costs for changing suppliers

#### Force 4: Threat of Substitutes

**Key principles**:
- Not always obvious whether a firm is a substitute or internal rival — structure analysis to capture real threats
- Think in detail about **who customers are** and **how they weigh attractiveness** of substitutes
- Example: LTL trucking customers may not find full-load trucking or rail attractive for small, rapid shipments

#### Force 5 (Sixth Force): Complementors

> "The five forces analysis suggests everything is a threat and everyone is your enemy. But often there are large gains to working collaboratively."

- Complementors increase value created in the market but may seek to capture that value for themselves
- Example: Apple legitimized online music sales to drive iPod sales, not to help labels earn high profits
- DRAM manufacturers share IP to develop new generations of technology, even though they compete
- **Understand partner motivations** and think about future market evolution

### 1.3 Remedies to Problematic Industry Structure

| Threat | Prospective Remedies |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Rivalry & Substitutes** | Differentiate product; merge with rivals; partner with rivals; change markets |
| **Entry Threat** | Get there first (expand range); control key resources; change perception of viability/profitability of entry |
| **Buyer & Supplier Power** | Lower differentiation of suppliers/buyers; vertically integrate; partner more closely; change channels/markets |

### 1.4 Case Applications — Industry Profit Potential

- **LTL Trucking (circa 2001)**: Entry barriers differ from full-load trucking; substitutes (FTL, rail) pose varying threats; five forces analysis of price increase sustainability
- **Costco Vertical Integration (Rotisserie Chicken)**: Strategic loss leader; supplier market failing Costco; Nebraska location rationale; risks of vertical integration
- **Netflix**: DVD mail-order success (tech change to DVDs), streaming transition, Disney licensing moves, WWE deal assessment
- **US Farmers**: Five forces analysis of how farmers improve profits against competitive threats

## MODULE 2: Precise Positioning

### 2.1 Value Creation

**Competitive Advantage** = ability to drive a wider wedge between benefits and costs than competitors:

$$\text{Value Created} = B - C$$
$$= (B - \text{Price}) + (\text{Price} - C)$$
$$= \text{Net Consumer Benefit} + \text{Net Profit}$$

Where:
- **B** = Consumer's willingness to pay (benefit)
- **C** = Firm's cost to deliver

**Key insight**: It's not just about making B large — it's about making B−C positive. Quantity of consumers also matters.

**MoviePass lesson**: High B for consumers, but MoviePass's cost to acquire tickets at face value made B−C negative for MoviePass. AMC can offer similar service because AMC's true cost C is lower than face value.

**Netflix vs. Blockbuster**: Netflix created value through greater content selection (not new releases), easier search process, and cost-efficient central warehouse distribution (vs. prime B&M real estate).

### 2.2 Resource-Based View (RBV) and VRIN

**RBV**: All profits ultimately emanate from the resources of a firm. Sustainable profits require resources that are "special."

**VRIN Framework** — Resources must be:
- **Valuable** — Contributes to meeting consumer desires?
- **Rare** — Supply/availability meaningfully limited?
- **Inimitable** — Challenging or prohibitively costly to replicate or acquire?
- **Non-substitutable** — Could a different resource get the job done?

**Broad definition of resources**: Not merely physical assets — includes expertise, IP, corporate culture, data, relationships.

**Three ways to use RBV/VRIN**:
1. **Assess** your resources according to VRIN (know what drives competitive advantage)
2. **Acquire/exploit/protect/sustain** VRIN resources (e.g., Facebook's ad-tech systems)
3. **Think dynamically** — VRIN characteristics change over time (Netflix DVD resources vs. streaming; Apple moving into semiconductor design)

**RBV also predicts competitor behavior**: Resources influence capabilities, which constrain actions. Car rental companies fighting Zipcar stored fleets at existing (less convenient) lots rather than leasing downtown parking spots.

### 2.3 Strategic Tradeoffs

> "Even excellent firms constrain themselves when they make strategic choices."

**Core principles**:
- Choosing a strategy requires choosing resources → choosing resources constrains what a firm can/will do
- Understand the difference between what rivals **"might do"** vs. what they **"will likely do"**
- **Exploit weaknesses** inherent in rival's strategic positioning
- The prototypical tradeoff: cost leadership vs. differentiation — hard to do both

**Strategic Tradeoffs are DEEPER than just "differentiate"**:
- Great strategies take advantage of strategic **limitations** of rivals
- Rivals may be limited by resources they control (or don't) or by aspects of their primary value-creation strategy
- Ask: "Do my resources and strategy make it challenging for a rival to respond?"

**Cost-Quality Frontier**: Useful visualization of tradeoffs. Replace cost/quality with other dimensions as needed (e.g., Intel: processing power vs. energy efficiency).

**Key examples**:
- Walmart: low-cost strategy exposes it to attacks on quality/differentiation
- ARM vs. Intel: different architectural tradeoffs
- Netflix vs. Blockbuster: Blockbuster constrained by B&M investment
- Athletic Brewing: exploiting beer giants' inability to credibly pursue NA segment

### 2.4 Internal and External Consistency

- **Internal consistency**: Alignment of resources and strategy throughout the organization
  - Gucci example: investments in retailing + manufacturing aligned with fashion-forward brand
  - "Bundle" of activities working together forms coherent strategy → hard for rivals to imitate unless they imitate ALL key activities
- **External consistency**: Strategy situated to exploit the external economic environment, especially changes
  - Gucci example: fashion magazines promoting fast fashion in 1990s → Gucci adjusted strategy to capture this shift

### 2.5 Positional Risks

Think about the **longer term** when assessing a firm's market position. Changes in technology, regulations, and consumer behavior can erode competitive advantage.

**Examples**:
- **Apple PCs (1980s)**: Closed system → high differentiation and profits initially, but ceded mass market to IBM's open system. Software developers and peripheral manufacturers gravitated to IBM PC. Key differentiators (GUI) eventually copied.
- **Gucci under Tom Ford**: Aimed for middle market + fast fashion rather than competing with Chanel/Hermès directly. Captured upside when fast fashion became popular. A gamble that paid off.

### 2.6 Case Applications — Precise Positioning

- **Athletic Brewing**: NA beer 26% CAGR; VRIN advantages in brewing expertise + branding + alternative distribution channels vs. beer giants
- **Tesla**: Competitive advantage through battery tech, software, brand, Supercharger network
- **Satellite Radio (XM/Sirius)**: Resources and value creation vs. terrestrial radio; ultimate merger
- **Walmart in Groceries**: Natural entrant due to distribution + logistics VRIN; strategic tradeoffs in quality
- **Shopify**: Value creation for merchants; VRIN in platform technology; advice on central traffic webpage
- **Target "Stores as Hubs"**: Physical stores as VRIN for omnichannel; connection between value creation and resources/investments
- **Shein**: VRIN in real-time supply chain tech; strategic move to market technology to other brands

## MODULE 3: Structuring for Success

### 3.1 Organizational Structure Principles

> "Strategy and organizational structure are closely intertwined. Both are choices firms make."

**Key questions**: How should authority/responsibility be allocated? Which corporate culture is best? Should the firm pursue M&A? What should be the extent of vertical integration?

**Firms solve problems that markets alone cannot.** A firm is a collection of resources organized according to self-determined rules and power structures.

**P&G reorganization (circa 1999)**: Shifted from regional managers across all product lines → global product-line focused units. Addressed low innovation rates, need to strengthen global brands, and stodgy culture.

### 3.2 Mergers & Acquisitions

**Empirical reality**: Many studies suggest M&A isn't profitable for the acquirer. Possible reasons: bad advice, overestimated synergies, agency problems (management compensated for short-term), ego.

**Fundamental M&A principle**: For M&A to make sense, the combined company must be able to **do new things or do old things better**. If no changes occur, no value is created. If no value created and target is properly valued, you don't profit on average.

**Two main classes of good justifications**:

#### A. Synergies/Efficiencies ("Make a bigger pie")

**Revenue synergies**:
- Cross-selling products into each other's markets (AB InBev + SABMiller in Africa)
- Using resources from one firm to improve another (Gucci + YSL with Tom Ford)
- More compelling product (airline route mergers)
- Innovation from combining companies

**Cost synergies**:
- Economies of scale (consolidating production)
- Eliminating redundancies (HQ, management, IT)
- Coordinated purchasing (buying in bulk)
- Sharing superior know-how

#### B. Market Power ("Take a bigger piece of the pie")

- Increased power over buyers
- Firms in related markets competing less fiercely once co-owned
- Requires firms to have **non-trivial market share** already
- Denying a firm to a rival (Facebook buying Instagram/WhatsApp)
- Reducing future R&D competition (Bayer + Monsanto)

**Diversion ratio test**: "If Firm X raised prices 5%, how much demand goes to Firm Y?" When ratio is high, merger to raise prices is more profitable.

**Dis-synergies**: Ruining something good by aiming for synergies. Example: Sears acquisition of Lands' End — Sears' brand and retail approach undermined what made Lands' End successful.

**Warehouse Synergy Example**: Two shipping firms with warehouses in similar locations, each 30% full. Merger creates real synergy: consolidate into fewer warehouses, eliminate redundancy, and improve utilization.

#### Common but QUESTIONABLE justifications:
- "For cash" → you always pay the value of cash
- "To diversify risk" → less expensive ways exist (financial instruments)

#### Natural Ownership Concept
Some given target firm C might be better owned by Firm A than by Firm B — perhaps A has more experience with C's products or better managers who understand C's operations.

### 3.3 Four Questions to Ask About Any Deal

1. **Does this deal allow you to create or capture more value net of the acquisition price?**
2. **Would organic growth or a partnership be better than M&A?**
3. **Is there a good fit with your target?** (Culture, values, strategic/operational alignment, key leaders)
4. **Are there any risks or possible unintended consequences?** (Integration issues, dis-synergies)

### 3.4 Vertical Integration

**Four common types of VI**:
1. **Lower costs or "snatch margins"** — Take margins from upstream/downstream players (doesn't always work)
2. **Realize synergies with partners** — Better coordination, information sharing (Zara: owning production + retail for fast fashion responsiveness)
3. **Limit opportunistic behavior** — Prevent hold-up problems; encourage team-oriented play when separate ownership creates misaligned incentives
4. **Deny key resources to rivals (input foreclosure)** — Must be hard for rivals to find alternate sources; may lose sales of input (Samsung sells chips to Apple because it's more profitable than foreclosing)

**VI considerations**:
- Just because VI is possible doesn't mean it's credible — factor in all costs
- Economies of scale in supply market may cause cost disadvantage for integrating firm
- Contracts, alliances, or joint ventures may achieve similar benefits with lower cost/risk
- Asset specificity: when customization limits future bargaining power, VI or equity stakes may be needed (Intel + Cloudera)
- Costco chicken: about more than unit costs — about supply chain control, product specification, strategic importance

### 3.5 Case Applications — Structuring for Success

- **Facebook/Instagram (2012, $1B)**: Synergies + market power; preserving dominance against emerging photo-sharing threat; PEST analysis (tech + social drivers)
- **Alaska Air/Virgin America**: Route network synergies vs. market power (overlapping routes = more market power, less synergy)
- **Amazon/Whole Foods**: Market power and synergy effects; organic growth alternative; strategic fit assessment
- **JetBlue/Spirit (blocked)**: Judge ruled elimination of low-cost carrier reduces innovation and price discipline
- **AOL/Time Warner**: Cautionary tale — related businesses ≠ unlocked synergies; cultural mismatch; overvalued stock driving acquisitions
- **Gen AI investments**: Microsoft/OpenAI, Microsoft/Inflection AI, Amazon/Anthropic — synergy and innovation vs. market power?
- **Food Giants + Organic Companies (Group Project)**: Danone/WhiteWave, Hormel/Applegate, General Mills/Annie's — VRIN analysis, synergies, market power, integration risks

## MODULE 4: Game Theory — Strategic Interaction

### 4.1 Core Concepts

**Opening Examples**:
- **Wargames (1983)**: The movie illustrates a **credibility/commitment problem** — in nuclear deterrence (MAD), the "game" leads to mutual destruction unless someone changes the game. The "zany solution" (teaching the computer tic-tac-toe) illustrates that sometimes the only winning move is not to play.
- **Patent Trolls & High-Tech Détente**: Typical tech companies exist in a détente — "If I sue a rival, they will countersue me. It's best not to sue." Patent trolls break this because they don't manufacture anything, so they can't be countersued. This illustrates the power of understanding the players, the game, and how to change the game.

**A game requires three inputs**:
1. **Players**: Who influences outcomes and has something to gain/lose?
2. **Actions**: What options does each player choose from?
3. **Payoffs**: For each combination of actions, what outcomes for each player?

**Equilibrium**: Combination of actions where no player wants to change their action given others don't change. Represents stability in incentives.

**Dominant Strategy**: Action a player wants to take no matter what others do. If opponent has dominant strategy → easy to predict but hard to influence.

### 4.2 Understanding Competitors

Three dimensions to understand about rivals:
- **Resources/Capabilities**: Shape what they can/cannot do (excess capacity, cash reserves → ability to retaliate)
- **Assumptions**: Competitor's assumptions about the game and your behavior impact their actions
- **Motivation**: Not all firms optimize the same way (present vs. future profits, market share vs. perceived value, social impact vs. profitability)

**Strategic Narcissism** (H.R. McMaster): "The tendency to define challenges as we would like them to be and to pay too little attention to the agency that others have over the future." (eBay vs. Craigslist example)

### 4.3 Classic Games

**Prisoners' Dilemma**: Both players have dominant strategies that leave them worse off than cooperation. Example: Uber vs. Lyft recruiting drivers — both would benefit from recruiting new drivers, but each has incentive to steal from the other.

**Game of Chicken (War of Attrition)**: Winner-take-all market. Typical strategies include commitment devices. BSkyB formation (1986). Question: Is leading AI model development a winner-take-all market?

**Ferocious Pirates**: Sequential game illustrating backward induction. Three pirates splitting 100 gold coins with majority voting. Teaches sub-game perfect equilibrium (SPE).

### 4.4 Dynamic Games and Influencing Others

**Sequential games** mean past/future actions can influence rival behavior.

**Conditions for successful signaling**:
1. Opponent must have incentive to take the action you want IF your signal is true
2. Your signal must be **credible**: you must have incentive to follow through

**Establishing credibility through investment**:
- Tesla Gigafactory: irreversible commitment (vs. VW's claim "we are all-in on EV")
- Google Fiber: big flop or credible message?
- Investments change payoffs from future actions and directly influence others

### 4.5 Strategic Postures

**Evil Orc** (Tough/Brutal):
- Make life miserable for competitors; grab all opportunities; respond aggressively
- Examples: Microsoft (product expansion + bundling), Intel (CPU rationing), Amazon ($15 minimum wage challenge)

**Fat Cat** (Conciliatory):
- Give rivals enough to not compete fiercely; seek mutual advantage
- Example: Coke and Pepsi coexistence

### 4.6 Co-opetition and Added Value

**Added Value**: The additional economic surplus created when you add a player to the game.

**Outside Option**: What you get if you exit a particular game (opportunity cost of playing).

**Maximizing profits often means**:
- Raising your own added value
- Raising your outside option
- Lowering the added value or outside option of others

**Card Game**: With equal numbers of red and black cards, each card-holder has equal added value → competitive pricing. Remove one black card → that holder's added value increases dramatically.

**Zero-Sum to Win-Win**: A zero-sum game is one in which your gain is my loss — inherently confrontational. But sometimes zero-sum games can be transformed to win-win scenarios. Co-opetition reminds us to look for win-win opportunities while being aware that partners want a piece of the pie.

**"Evil Sister" Example (Win-Win to Zero-Sum)**: Two sisters splitting an orange. If one cares only about the juice and the other only about the rind, there's a win-win split. But if one sister is "evil" and knows the other's preference, she can exploit that knowledge to capture more value — transforming a win-win into a zero-sum.

**Music Industry circa 2002**: Apple as complementor to labels — legitimized online sales but used low-price music to drive iPod sales (capturing value for itself, not labels).

### 4.7 PARTS Framework (Changing the Game)

| Element | Question | Case Examples |
|---------|----------|---------------|
| **Players** | Do we want to add/remove players? | NutraSweet & Holland Sweetener (HSC); increase supplier competition; consolidation |
| **Added Values** | How to make ourselves more indispensable? | Card Game (removing a card = monopoly power); commitment devices (burning cards, redesigning planes) |
| **Rules** | Can we alter the mode of competition? | Kiwi Airlines (reduced retaliation risk); MCC/Most-Favored Customer clauses; price matching guarantees |
| **Tactics** | Can we change perceptions? | New York Post (lifting/increasing uncertainty); Ryanair "celebrating" Aer Lingus birthday |
| **Scope** | Will today's move influence future games? | Sega 16-bit system (cross-game repercussions); reputation effects across markets |

**Case examples**:
- **Holland Sweetener vs. NutraSweet**: HSC failed to understand NutraSweet's position; how could they have dealt better with Coke and Pepsi?
- **Kiwi Airlines**: Managed to reduce risk of retaliation
- **Ryanair "celebrating" Aer Lingus birthday**: Strategic message to influence competitor perception

### 4.8 The Value Net

Organized along two dimensions:
- **Supply chain axis**: upstream (suppliers) vs. downstream (buyers)
- **Cooperation/competition axis**: from substitutors to complementors

Helps maintain an up-to-date list of all players whose actions are relevant to success.

### 4.9 Analytical Tools

**Matrix** (simultaneous games): Circle best response for each player given each opponent action. Equilibrium = box where both are circled.

**Tree** (sequential games): Use **backward induction** — start at last decision point, work back to root. Result = **Sub-game Perfect Equilibrium (SPE)**. Rules out non-credible behavior.

## Five Forces + Game Theory Integration

Five Forces provides the **diagnosis** (which competitive forces are most problematic); Game Theory provides the **prescription** (how to act strategically given those forces). This section bridges the two frameworks with a worked quantitative example and practical mappings.

> **Cross-references:**
> - See **Microeconomics.SKILL.md** for formal game theory tools (Nash Equilibrium solving methods, repeated games, collusion dynamics, Bertrand/Cournot models)
> - See **Negotiation.SKILL.md** for bargaining theory applications (BATNA, interest-based negotiation, zone of possible agreement)

### Worked Payoff Matrix: Streaming Video Pricing Game

**Scenario**: Netflix and Disney+ are deciding quarterly pricing strategy. Each can set a Low Price (aggressive, market-share-grabbing) or High Price (margin-protecting). Payoffs represent quarterly profit in $B.

```
Netflix vs. Disney+ Pricing Game (quarterly profit in $B)

                     Disney+ Low Price    Disney+ High Price
Netflix Low Price    (2.0, 1.5)           (3.5, 0.8)
Netflix High Price   (1.2, 2.8)           (3.0, 2.5)
```

**Step-by-step Nash Equilibrium analysis:**

1. **Netflix's best responses:**
   - If Disney+ chooses Low Price: Netflix earns 2.0 (Low) vs. 1.2 (High) → **Low is better**
   - If Disney+ chooses High Price: Netflix earns 3.5 (Low) vs. 3.0 (High) → **Low is better**
   - Netflix has a **dominant strategy: Low Price**

2. **Disney+'s best responses:**
   - If Netflix chooses Low Price: Disney+ earns 1.5 (Low) vs. 0.8 (High) → **Low is better**
   - If Netflix chooses High Price: Disney+ earns 2.8 (Low) vs. 2.5 (High) → **Low is better**
   - Disney+ has a **dominant strategy: Low Price**

3. **Nash Equilibrium: Both choose Low Price → (2.0, 1.5)**
   - Combined industry profit at equilibrium: $3.5B
   - Combined industry profit if both chose High Price: $5.5B
   - **Value destroyed by the price war: $2.0B per quarter**

4. **This is a Prisoner's Dilemma:**
   - The cooperative outcome (Both High: $3.0B + $2.5B = $5.5B) is better for **both** players
   - But each player has an individual incentive to undercut, making Low Price dominant
   - **Strategic implication: Price wars are value-destroying; firms should seek mechanisms to sustain cooperative outcomes**

5. **Escape strategies (from Game Theory MODULE 4):**
   - Repeated interaction enables tacit cooperation (tit-for-tat)
   - Signaling commitment to high prices (e.g., public statements, long-term content investments)
   - Differentiating content to reduce head-to-head price competition
   - Using PARTS framework to change the game structure entirely

### Five Forces Diagnosis → Game Theory Prescription

Each problematic Five Forces finding maps to a specific game-theoretic tool:

| Five Forces Finding | Game Theory Prescription | Key Mechanism |
|---------------------|--------------------------|---------------|
| **High Rivalry** | Prisoner's Dilemma framework | Identify whether price/capacity competition is a PD; seek repeated-game cooperation or differentiation to escape |
| **Threat of Entry** | Entry Deterrence Game (sequential) | Incumbent moves first — commit to capacity expansion, aggressive pricing reputation, or patent walls to make entry unprofitable (credible commitment required) |
| **Supplier Power** | Bargaining Game | Supplier's outside option = their BATNA; reduce it by developing alternative suppliers, backward integrating, or forming purchasing coalitions |
| **Buyer Power** | Price Discrimination strategies | Segment buyers, version products, bundle offerings — extract surplus from high-WTP buyers while competing for price-sensitive ones |
| **Substitutes** | Value Migration analysis | Model the switching threshold: at what price/quality gap do customers switch? Invest to keep the gap above the switching threshold |

**Detailed mappings:**

**High Rivalry → Prisoner's Dilemma:**
- When firms compete on price with undifferentiated products, the payoff structure often resembles a PD
- Diagnosis: map out the actual payoff matrix (as in the streaming example above)
- Prescription: repeated games allow tacit cooperation; signaling and commitment devices (PARTS: Tactics) help sustain it
- Warning: if the game is one-shot or competitors are irrational, cooperation is unlikely

**Threat of Entry → Entry Deterrence Game:**
- This is a **sequential game**: the incumbent moves first (invest in capacity, set prices, build brand), then the entrant decides whether to enter
- Incumbent's investment must be **credible** (sunk costs, irreversible commitments)
- Example: Tesla Gigafactory signals massive production commitment → raises the bar for EV entrants
- Use backward induction to determine whether the entrant will still find entry profitable after the incumbent's commitment

**Supplier Power → Bargaining Game:**
- Supplier's leverage depends on their **outside option** (BATNA) relative to yours
- Reduce supplier power by: developing alternative suppliers (adding Players), backward integration threats (credible commitment), or long-term contracts (changing Rules)
- The bargaining outcome depends on each side's added value in the relationship

**Buyer Power → Price Discrimination:**
- When buyers have power, uniform pricing leaves money on the table
- Segment customers by willingness to pay and offer tiered products (versioning)
- Bundle complementary products to increase switching costs and reduce price transparency
- Example: Software companies offering Enterprise/Pro/Free tiers extract surplus from high-WTP corporate buyers while competing for individuals

**Substitutes → Value Migration:**
- Model the critical threshold where customers switch to substitutes
- Invest to maintain the value gap above the switching threshold
- Monitor substitute improvements and respond preemptively
- Example: Cable TV needed to monitor at what price/content gap streaming became preferable — they largely failed to respond in time

### Using PARTS to Change the Game

The PARTS framework (MODULE 4.7) is the operational bridge from diagnosis to action. Each element offers levers to restructure the competitive game:

| PARTS Element | How It Changes the Game | Strategic Levers |
|---------------|------------------------|------------------|
| **Players** | Add or remove participants to shift the balance of power | Add complementors to increase total value; acquire rivals to reduce rivalry; invite new suppliers to reduce supplier power |
| **Added Values** | Increase your indispensability; make customers sticky | Build switching costs (data lock-in, ecosystem integration); create network effects; develop VRIN resources that raise your added value |
| **Rules** | Alter the formal structure of competition | Set industry standards, use contracts (MFN clauses, exclusivity), lobby for favorable regulation, establish price-matching guarantees |
| **Tactics** | Change information and perceptions | Signal commitment (irreversible investments), reveal or conceal information strategically, shape competitor expectations through public actions |
| **Scope** | Link the current game to other games across markets or time | Bundle across markets, build reputation that carries across interactions, use today's moves to influence tomorrow's competitive landscape |

**PARTS application to the streaming pricing example:**
- **Players**: Netflix partners with telecom bundlers (complementors) to increase subscriber stickiness
- **Added Values**: Invest in exclusive original content (Stranger Things, Squid Game) that only Netflix offers — raises Netflix's added value in the market
- **Rules**: Long-term content licensing deals lock up key IP, changing the rules of content acquisition
- **Tactics**: Publicly announce content spending budgets ($17B/year) to signal commitment and deter price wars
- **Scope**: Expand into gaming and merchandise to link the streaming game to adjacent markets, increasing overall platform value

## MODULE 5: Network Effects and Platform Strategy

### 5.1 Platform Definition

A platform is a product/service that **intermediates multiple different sides** of a market, profiting by charging one or more sides.

Examples: Videogame consoles (gamers ↔ developers), Facebook (users ↔ advertisers), Uber (passengers ↔ drivers), Netflix (viewers ↔ content owners)

### 5.2 Types of Network Effects

**Same-Side Network Effects**: Players care about how many players are on "their side."
- Phone networks, MS Word compatibility, WhatsApp

**Cross-Side Network Effects**: Players care about how many players are on "the other side."
- iOS users want more iOS apps, Uber passengers want more drivers, gamers want more developers

Most platforms exhibit BOTH types.

### 5.3 Platform Economics

> "With network effects—especially cross-side—the usual logic of economics changes."

- Facebook charges users nothing because user growth drives advertiser value (cross-side)
- **Cannot analyze each side in isolation**
- Most profitable companies in the world are platforms (Google, Meta, Apple) — WHY? Because network effects create massive entry barriers

### 5.4 Platform Strategy Principles

Three broad strategic goals:
1. **Create and sustain a trusted, vibrant ecosystem**
2. **Create low incentives for participants to switch platforms**
3. **Create low incentives for competitors to invest**

### 5.5 Chicken-or-Egg Problem

Getting a platform started is hard — why join if no one else has? Due to network effects, initial success may be long-lasting, but getting the ball rolling is the challenge.

**Strategies**: Subsidize one side, create compelling standalone value, leverage existing user bases, acquire content/users

**TikTok's Cold Start Solution**: TikTok solved the chicken-or-egg problem through algorithmic content discovery that didn't require an existing social graph — new users got immediate value from curated short-form video.

**Playing Catch-Up — Instagram Reels**: TikTok revealed that short-form video drives massive engagement. Facebook/Instagram was late to short-form video, introducing Reels in 2020. Instagram made significant changes to its own product to help grow Reels — demonstrating how incumbents must sometimes reshape their core product to compete with platform challengers.

### 5.6 Open vs. Closed Systems

**MySpace vs. Facebook**: MySpace tried to build every feature internally. Facebook used Open Graph APIs to let developers innovate → drove customer acquisition and engagement → drove ad spend.

**Facebook's pivot to closed (circa 2015)**: After establishing dominance through openness, Facebook began denying API access to prevent rivals from using the platform's network effects. Denied Vine (Twitter's video app) access. **Timing is key**: use openness to establish dominance, then close to protect it.

### 5.7 Apple ATT (App Tracking Transparency)

Demonstrates platform power to design rules that dramatically impact market outcomes. Possible motivations beyond consumer privacy: competitive advantage in advertising, undermining competitors.

### 5.8 Consulting Firm Example

Jason Ozark and Mitchell Spain leaving Sundial Consulting (owned by Acme International) to start Epitome Consulting. Illustrates chicken-or-egg and platform dynamics in professional services: need Rainmakers (rare) to attract clients, need clients to attract Soldiers.

### Modified Pirate Game (Q24-Q26)
Setup: Three pirates (A, B, C) split 100 coins. A proposes first, majority vote (tie = pass). If rejected, proposer thrown overboard (-1 payoff). **Modification**: Pirate B believes in fairness — votes against any proposal where any pirate gets 2+ more coins than another. B also proposes fairly. This is commonly known.

24. **The most Pirate A can get is 34 coins** → TRUE — A must propose within B's fairness threshold: 34/33/33 is the maximum A can get while securing B's vote
25. **Pirate C will receive at least 50 coins** → FALSE — C receives 33 in equilibrium (via backward induction: A proposes 34/33/33, B votes yes)
26. **B would receive more if A didn't know B believes in fairness** → TRUE — if A thinks B is selfish, A proposes 98/0/1 (standard pirate game); B rejects (fairness), A thrown overboard; then B proposes 50/50 to C. B gets 50 > 33
